# Episode 6: What If You Found a Five-Leaf Clover Grimoire?

A what-if inspired by the anime idea of a five-leaf grimoire with an anti-magic sword, told with our own hero and our own designs (no characters, names or art from any anime). One continuous story about YOU, present tense: you find a five-leaf clover and a black spell book climbs out of the ground; it gives you a huge black sword you can't lift; you train; you find out the sword cancels things; a thunderstorm at a football match, where you cancel a lightning bolt; and a twist. Real science all the way through: clover odds and genes, the science of luck, real sword weights, how strength training works, noise cancelling, antimatter, lightning and Faraday cages. One narrator (Kokoro "Heart"). Speech appears on screen; nobody else speaks out loud.

How it's drawn: normal scale. A park full of clover, your house and bedroom, the school and its football field, a studio for the science. The grimoire is a thick black book with gold corners and a five-leaf clover on the cover, trailing black smoke. The sword is black, chipped and nearly as tall as you.

Facts used, and where they come from:
- White clover (Trifolium repens) leaves normally have three leaflets. Four-leaf clovers: commonly estimated at about 1 in 10,000; a large count often cited puts it nearer 1 in 5,000. Five-leaf clovers are rarer still.
- Tashiro, Parrott and colleagues, University of Georgia, Crop Science (2010): mapped the genes linked to four-leaf clovers; the trait is strongly influenced by the environment, so a plant with the genes doesn't always show it.
- Guinness World Records (2024): most leaves on a clover, 63, grown by Yoshiharu Watanabe of Nasushiobara, Japan. (The previous record, 56 leaves, was Shigeo Obara's, 2009.)
- Richard Wiseman (University of Hertfordshire), The Luck Factor (2003) and Scientific American: asked self-described lucky and unlucky people to count the photographs in a newspaper; page two had a half-page message, "Stop counting. There are 43 photographs in this newspaper." Unlucky people took about two minutes to count; lucky people took seconds, because they noticed the message.
- Real medieval swords were light: most longswords about 1 to 1.5 kg; in the Wallace Collection catalogue it is hard to find any sword over about 1.8 kg (4 lb). A solid steel blade 1.6 m long, 25 cm wide and 1 cm thick: 0.004 m³ × 7,850 kg/m³ ≈ 31 kg, about the weight of an average nine-year-old.
- Moritani and deVries, American Journal of Physical Medicine (1979), "Neural factors versus hypertrophy in the time course of muscle strength gain": in the first weeks of strength training most of the gain comes from the nervous system learning to use the muscle; muscle growth takes over later. Muscle is rebuilt during recovery after training.
- Paul Lueg patented active noise cancelling (cancelling a sound with an opposite wave) in the 1930s (US patent 2,043,416, granted 1936). Noise-cancelling headphones use the same idea.
- Paul Dirac's equation (1928) predicted the anti-electron; Carl Anderson found it (the positron) in a cloud chamber in 1932 (Nobel Prize 1936). A particle and its antiparticle annihilate into energy.
- A banana's potassium-40 gives off a positron roughly once every 75 minutes (Symmetry magazine, Fermilab/SLAC, 2009). PET (positron emission tomography) scanners in hospitals use positrons.
- US National Weather Service: lightning heats the air around it to as much as about 28,000 °C (50,000 °F), about five times hotter than the surface of the Sun; it can strike up to about 10 miles (16 km) from a storm; "When thunder roars, go indoors."
- Flash to bang: sound travels about 343 m a second, roughly 1 km every 3 seconds, so counting the seconds between the flash and the thunder and dividing by 3 gives the distance in kilometres (divide by 5 for miles). National Weather Service: if you can hear thunder, you are close enough to be struck.
- National Weather Service: the odds of being struck by lightning in the US in a given year are about 1 in 1.2 million; over a lifetime of 80 years, about 1 in 15,300.
- SKYbrary / Flight Safety Foundation: a passenger airliner is struck by lightning about once every 1,000 flight hours, roughly once a year; the current runs along the metal skin and out again (the Faraday cage idea). Michael Faraday (1836) built a room coated in metal foil, struck the outside with high-voltage sparks and showed there was no charge inside. Cars protect you because of their metal body, not their rubber tyres.

### clover
*Science: a sea of clover from above. A counter runs up; one clover glows gold: FOUR LEAVES · ABOUT 1 IN 5,000 TO 10,000. Then a single five-leaf clover: FIVE LEAVES · EVEN RARER.*
NARRATOR: Five-leaf clovers are real, by the way. A white clover leaf normally has three little leaflets. Pick thousands of them, and you might find one with four. Most counts say it's somewhere around one in five thousand to one in ten thousand. And five leaves is rarer still.

### gene
*A clover with a twisting DNA strand behind it: CROP SCIENCE · 2010 · UNIVERSITY OF GEORGIA. Then two plants, one in sun and one in shade: same genes, different leaves.*
NARRATOR: In twenty ten, a team at the University of Georgia found the genes behind the extra leaves. But genes aren't the whole story. A clover with those genes can still grow just three leaves, depending on where it grows, and how warm, wet or sunny it is. So the luck is partly in the plant, and partly in the weather.

### luck
*Studio: you and your friend, each counting photos in a newspaper. A huge message on page two: STOP COUNTING. THERE ARE 43 PHOTOGRAPHS IN THIS NEWSPAPER. Your friend keeps counting; you spot it and grin. RICHARD WISEMAN · THE LUCK FACTOR.*
NARRATOR: So, does a clover make you lucky? A psychologist called Richard Wiseman studied people who said they were lucky, and people who said they weren't. He gave them a newspaper, and asked them to count the photos inside. But on page two, in huge letters, it said: stop counting, there are forty three photographs in this newspaper. The lucky people usually spotted it in seconds. The unlucky people were so busy counting, they often missed it.
[pause 0.3]
NARRATOR: Luck, it turns out, is partly just noticing things.

### heavy
*You drag the sword home by the handle, cutting a groove up the garden path. Mum at the door: "WHERE did you get THAT?" Then the studio: a real longsword on a scale, about 1.5 kg, next to a bag of sugar. Your sword on a scale that squashes flat: ABOUT 30 KG.*
NARRATOR: You drag the sword home. It leaves a groove all the way up the path. Here's the thing about swords in films and comics. Real ones were surprisingly light. Most medieval longswords weighed around one to one and a half kilograms, about as much as a bag of sugar. But a solid slab of steel the size of yours would weigh about thirty kilograms. That's like swinging a whole nine year old.

### train
*Training montage at dawn: push-ups in the garden, squats, sprints round the park with the sword strapped to your back. A calendar flips: DAY 1, the tip barely lifts. DAY 10, up to your knees. DAY 30, you swing it over your head.*
NARRATOR: So you train. Every morning, before school. Push ups. Squats. Sprints around the park, with the sword strapped to your back. On day one, you can just about lift the tip. By day ten, you can lift it to your knees. And by day thirty, you can swing it right over your head.
[hold 0.4]

### muscles
*Science: a cartoon arm. In the first weeks, the nerves light up: YOUR BRAIN LEARNS TO USE THE MUSCLE. Later, the muscle itself grows. MORITANI & DEVRIES · 1979. Then you asleep: MUSCLE IS REBUILT WHILE YOU REST.*
NARRATOR: And this part is real. When you start lifting heavy things, you get stronger surprisingly fast. But at first, it's not because your muscles are bigger. In nineteen seventy nine, two scientists, Toshio Moritani and Herbert de Vries, found that in the first few weeks, most of the gain comes from your brain and nerves learning to use the muscle you already have. The muscle itself grows later. And it rebuilds while you rest, not while you lift.
[hold 0.5]

### power
*Your bedroom at night. You carry the sword past your desk fan: it stops dead. Past your phone: the music cuts out. The blade touches your lamp: the light goes out. You pull it away, and everything comes back on.*
NARRATOR: Then, one night, you notice something strange. When the blade gets close to your desk fan, the fan stops. When it gets close to your phone, the music cuts out. When it touches your lamp, the light goes out. You pull the sword away, and everything comes back. The sword doesn't cut things. It cancels them.
[hold 0.5]

### noise
*Your classroom. Through the wall, the school band is practising: big wobbly sound waves come through, everyone covers their ears. You lift the sword an inch, and the waves go flat. Silence. Then the studio: a sound wave plus its upside-down twin makes a flat line. PAUL LUEG · 1930s.*
NARRATOR: The next day, the school band is practising next door, and it is loud. You lift the sword, just a little, and the whole room goes silent.
[pause 0.3]
NARRATOR: Cancelling sound is actually real. Sound is a wave, and if you make the exact opposite wave at the same moment, the two cancel out. That's how noise cancelling headphones work. A German physicist patented the idea back in the nineteen thirties, decades before the headphones existed.
[hold 0.5]

### anti
*Studio, dark. The word ANTI glows. A chalkboard equation: DIRAC · 1928. A curly particle track in a cloud chamber: ANDERSON · 1932 · THE POSITRON. A particle and its anti twin collide and vanish in a flash.*
NARRATOR: Your book's power, it seems, is anti. And anti is real too. In nineteen twenty eight, a physicist called Paul Dirac worked out that every particle should have an opposite twin, a mirror version with the opposite charge. Four years later, Carl Anderson photographed one, the anti electron, streaking through his cloud chamber. Today we call it a positron. And when a particle meets its anti twin, they both vanish in a flash of pure energy.
[hold 0.4]

### banana
*A banana with a tiny spark popping off it now and then: ABOUT 1 POSITRON EVERY 75 MINUTES. Then a hospital scanner ring with a person sliding in: PET SCANNER.*
NARRATOR: There's even some in your fruit bowl. Bananas are full of potassium, and a tiny bit of that potassium is radioactive. So an ordinary banana gives off a positron about once every seventy five minutes. Hospitals use positrons too, in pet scanners, to see what's happening inside people's bodies.
[hold 0.5]

### storm

NARRATOR: Then comes Saturday. Your friends have a big football match on the school field, and you've come to watch, with the sword on your back. Halfway through, the sky goes dark. Thunder rumbles across the field. And nobody stops playing.

### count
*The field. A flash lights up the sky. You count on your fingers: one, two, three, four, five, six. Then BOOM. A HUD: 6 SECONDS ÷ 3 = 2 KM AWAY. Sound waves ripple across the field.*
NARRATOR: There's a flash over the hills, and you start counting. One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Then the thunder hits. That's a real trick. Light reaches you almost instantly, but sound only travels about a kilometre every three seconds. So six seconds means the storm is about two kilometres away. And it's getting closer.
[hold 0.4]

### thunder
*Studio with storm clouds: a lightning bolt and the Sun side by side: LIGHTNING · ABOUT 28,000 °C. SUN'S SURFACE · ABOUT 5,500 °C. A map ring: CAN STRIKE 16 KM FROM THE STORM. Then a sign: WHEN THUNDER ROARS, GO INDOORS.*
NARRATOR: That's a real mistake. A bolt of lightning heats the air around it to nearly thirty thousand degrees, about five times hotter than the surface of the sun. And it can strike up to sixteen kilometres away from the storm. The real rule is simple. When thunder roars, go indoors.

### strike

The bolt hits the blade and stops, gone in a burst of black sparks. Silence. Then rain. Everyone stares.*
NARRATOR: You shout at everyone to get inside, and they start running. Then the sky flashes, and a bolt of lightning comes straight down towards your best friend. You don't even think. You jump, and swing the sword up over your head, exactly the way you've practised a thousand times. The lightning hits the blade, and just stops. It's gone. Cancelled.

### odds
*Studio with rain: a huge crowd of little figures, one highlighted: 1 IN 1.2 MILLION · EACH YEAR. Then a lifetime bar: 1 IN 15,300 OVER 80 YEARS. NATIONAL WEATHER SERVICE.*
NARRATOR: Getting struck by lightning is rare, but it's not as rare as you'd think. In the United States, the odds of being hit in any one year are about one in a million. But over a whole lifetime, it's about one in fifteen thousand. That's why the safest plan is never to rely on luck, or on a magic sword.
[hold 0.5]

### faraday
*Science: a plane flying through a storm is struck; the bolt runs along its metal skin and off the tail, and the passengers inside don't notice. ABOUT ONCE A YEAR. Then a foil-covered room with sparks outside: FARADAY · 1836. Then a car, the bolt running round its metal body: IT'S THE METAL, NOT THE TYRES.*
NARRATOR: In real life, nobody has an anti magic sword. But we do have something almost as good. A passenger plane gets hit by lightning roughly once a year. The electricity runs along the metal skin and out again, and the people inside are safe. It's called a Faraday cage, after Michael Faraday, who built a room covered in metal foil in eighteen thirty six, blasted the outside with sparks, and showed there was no charge inside. Cars work the same way. It's the metal body that protects you, not the rubber tyres.
[hold 0.5]
