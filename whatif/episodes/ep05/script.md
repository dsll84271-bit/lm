# Episode 5: What If You Could Fly?

One continuous story about YOU, present tense: the joy of flying, the real science of why people can't (muscles, bones, wing size), the people who flew on their own muscle power anyway, the speed, the cold and the thin air up high, getting lost, tired and sleepy over the sea, a climax where you use what birds know (soaring on warm air, flying in a V and taking turns) to get home, a twist, then a question for the comments. One narrator (Kokoro "Heart"). Speech appears on screen; nobody else speaks out loud.

How it's drawn: normal scale. Your town, the sky, the clouds, the mountains and the sea, with real birds (pigeon, peregrine falcon, bar-headed geese, godwit, frigatebird, condor) next to you. When you fly, you lie flat like a superhero, arms forward.

Facts used, and where they come from:
- Giovanni Alfonso Borelli, De Motu Animalium (1680): compared human and bird muscles and concluded human chest and arm muscles are far too weak to fly by flapping.
- Pigeon flight muscle: the pectoralis of an adult domestic pigeon is about 20% of its body mass (Hartman, 1961, Smithsonian Miscellaneous Collections; cited in pigeon flight studies as ">20% body mass").
- Elizabeth Dumont, Proceedings of the Royal Society B (2010), "Bone density and the lightweight skeletons of birds": bird bones are hollow but denser than mammal bones; bird skeletons weigh about the same as those of similar-sized mammals.
- Argentavis magnificens, about 6 million years ago, South America: largest known flying bird, about 70 kg, wingspan about 7 m. Chatterjee, Templin and Campbell, PNAS (2007): too heavy to take off or keep flying by flapping alone; it soared.
- Gossamer Albatross, 12 June 1979: Bryan Allen pedalled it across the English Channel, about 36 km in 2 hours 49 minutes, flying close to the water. Wingspan about 29 m; the empty aircraft weighed about 32 kg (less than its pilot).
- A typical cumulus cloud holds about 500 tonnes of water (about 1.1 million pounds): estimate by Peggy LeMone, National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR), reported by USGS and NCAR. About 100 elephants at 5 tonnes each.
- Birds navigate using the sun, the stars and Earth's magnetic field (well established in migration research).
- MIT Daedalus 88, 23 April 1988: Kanellos Kanellopoulos, a Greek cyclist, pedalled 115 km from Heraklion, Crete, to Santorini in 3 hours 55 minutes (FAI and Guinness record).
- Peregrine falcon: the fastest animal; dives at more than 300 km/h.
- Air temperature falls about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m you climb (standard atmosphere lapse rate).
- On the summit of Mount Everest (8,849 m), each breath holds about a third of the oxygen it would at sea level (air pressure about a third of sea level).
- Bar-headed geese migrate over the Himalayas. Hawkes and colleagues, Proceedings of the Royal Society B (2013), tracked 91 geese by GPS; the highest reading was 7,290 m. Their haemoglobin grabs oxygen more strongly than ours.
- Bar-tailed godwit 234684 (a juvenile) flew nonstop from Alaska to Tasmania in October 2022: about 13,560 km in 11 days, without stopping to eat or rest (Guinness World Records).
- Rattenborg and colleagues, Nature Communications (2016): great frigatebirds sleep in flight, sometimes with one half of the brain at a time, keeping the eye linked to the awake half open; about 42 minutes of sleep a day at sea.
- Hedenström and colleagues, Current Biology (2016): common swifts can stay airborne for 10 months without landing.
- Williams and colleagues, PNAS (2020): Andean condors, the heaviest soaring birds, flapped for only about 1% of their flight time; one flew more than 5 hours and about 172 km without flapping. Thermals are columns of rising warm air.
- Portugal and colleagues, Nature (2014): northern bald ibises in a V position themselves to catch the rising air (upwash) off the wingtip of the bird ahead, and time their wingbeats to it. Voelkl and colleagues, PNAS (2015): the ibises take turns at the front, matching the time each spends leading and following.

NARRATOR: You wake up with your nose pressed against the ceiling. Your duvet is on the floor. Your bed is a long way down. And you're not holding on to anything.

NARRATOR: You think, down, and you drift down. You think, up, and you float back up. You can fly.

### window
*You open the window and dive out. You shoot up over the rooftops. The street shrinks: houses like toys, cars like beetles. Wind in your hood.*
NARRATOR: So you do what anyone would do. You open the window, and you jump. The street drops away beneath you. The houses look like toys. The cars look like beetles. The wind roars in your ears, and you're laughing so hard you can barely breathe. You do a loop the loop over your own roof, and chase your shadow across the gardens.
[hold 0.5]

### school
*Over the traffic jam: the school bus stuck in a line of cars. You swoop low over it and land in the middle of the playground. Kids stare, then cheer: "NO WAY!"*
NARRATOR: Your walk to school usually takes twenty minutes. Today it takes two. You fly right over the traffic jam, over the school bus, over your teacher's car, and land in the middle of the playground. For a second, everyone just stares. Then everyone screams. Your best friend runs over and asks if you can teach them. You have absolutely no idea how.
[hold 0.5]

### flap
*Your friends try it: flapping their arms, jumping off a bench. Nothing. A pigeon takes off easily beside them.*
NARRATOR: Of course, at break time, everyone wants to try. Your friends flap their arms. They jump off benches. They jump off the climbing frame. Nobody leaves the ground for more than half a second. And a pigeon just flaps twice, and lifts off as if it's nothing.
[hold 0.5]

### borelli
*An old book: DE MOTU ANIMALIUM, GIOVANNI BORELLI, 1680, with a drawing of a man with feathered wings on his arms. Then a pigeon, its chest muscle glowing: FLIGHT MUSCLE: ABOUT 20% OF ITS WEIGHT.*
NARRATOR: Real people can't fly by flapping, and we've known why for a long time. Nearly three hundred and fifty years ago, an Italian scientist called Giovanni Borelli compared people with birds, and showed that our chest and arm muscles are far too weak to lift us.
[pause 0.3]
NARRATOR: A pigeon's main flight muscle alone is about a fifth of its whole weight. Yours are a tiny fraction of that.
[hold 0.5]

### bones
*An X-ray: a bird bone, hollow with little struts inside, next to a mammal bone. HOLLOW, BUT DENSER. DUMONT · 2010. Two skeletons on a scale, level.*
NARRATOR: You've probably heard that birds fly because they have hollow bones. Their bones are hollow. But in twenty ten, a scientist called Elizabeth Dumont found that they're also heavier for their size than ours. A bird's skeleton weighs about as much as a mammal's the same size. Light bones aren't the secret. Huge muscles, and the right wings, are.
[hold 0.5]

### giant
*A giant bird with enormous wings lands beside you, its wingspan longer than a car parked next to it. ARGENTAVIS · 6 MILLION YEARS AGO · ABOUT 70 KG · WINGS ABOUT 7 M.*
NARRATOR: So how big would your wings need to be? The biggest bird that ever flew, Argentavis, lived in South America six million years ago. It weighed about seventy kilograms, like a grown up person, and its wings stretched about seven metres, longer than a car. And even it was probably too heavy to keep itself up just by flapping. It soared.
[hold 0.5]

### gossamer
*A see-through pedal plane with huge thin wings skims a metre above grey sea waves. A cyclist pedals inside. GOSSAMER ALBATROSS · 1979 · ENGLISH CHANNEL · 36 KM. Then DAEDALUS · 1988 · CRETE → SANTORINI · 115 KM.*
NARRATOR: People have flown on their own muscle power, though. In nineteen seventy nine, a cyclist called Bryan Allen pedalled a plane called the Gossamer Albatross all the way across the English Channel. Its wings were nearly thirty metres long, and the whole plane weighed less than he did. It took him almost three hours, skimming just above the waves. Nine years later, a Greek cyclist went even further, pedalling a plane called Daedalus a hundred and fifteen kilometres, from Crete to the island of Santorini.
[hold 0.5]

### race
*Over the park. You race a pigeon and win. Then a peregrine falcon folds its wings and shoots past you like a missile. PEREGRINE DIVE: OVER 300 KM/H.*
NARRATOR: But you don't need any of that. After school, you race a pigeon across the park, and you win. The pigeon lands on a lamp post and glares at you. Then something shoots past you like a missile. A peregrine falcon, diving. Peregrines are the fastest animals on Earth. When they dive, they can go more than three hundred kilometres an hour.
[hold 0.5]

### clouds
*You fly straight up into a cloud: grey fog, droplets. You burst out of the top into sunshine. Below, a white sea of cloud stretches forever. A cloud on a scale against a herd of elephants: A PUFFY CLOUD ≈ 500 TONNES OF WATER.*
NARRATOR: Next, you want to see what's above the clouds. Inside a cloud, it's just cold, wet fog. Then you burst out of the top into sunshine, and there's a whole white world underneath you, like a field of snow that goes on forever.
[pause 0.3]
NARRATOR: That fluffy cloud you just flew through isn't light, either. Scientists at the National Center for Atmospheric Research in America worked out that an ordinary puffy cloud holds about five hundred tonnes of water. That's about as heavy as a hundred elephants, floating over your head as tiny droplets.
[hold 0.5]

### high
*Higher. Frost creeps over your hoodie. A thermometer drops. An oxygen meter empties. −6.5 °C EVERY 1,000 M. EVEREST: ⅓ OF THE OXYGEN.*
NARRATOR: You keep climbing, and it gets cold. Fast. Every kilometre you go up, the air gets about six and a half degrees colder. Frost grows on your hoodie. And no matter how hard you breathe, you can't get enough air. Your ears pop. Your fingers go numb.
[pause 0.3]
NARRATOR: The air up here is thin. At the top of Mount Everest, nearly nine kilometres up, every breath has only about a third of the oxygen you'd get at the beach.
[hold 0.5]

### geese
*Snowy mountain peaks poke through the clouds. A line of geese flies past you, calm. BAR-HEADED GOOSE: TRACKED AT 7,290 M.*
NARRATOR: And yet, something flies past you. Geese. Bar headed geese cross the Himalayas, the highest mountains on Earth, every year. When scientists tracked them, one was flying over seven thousand metres up. Their blood is built to grab oxygen out of thin air. Yours isn't. Your head spins, and you drop back below the clouds.
[hold 0.5]

### lost
*Below the clouds now: fields in every direction, a coast, the sea. No town you know. Your tummy rumbles. The sun is getting low.*
NARRATOR: That's when you notice two problems. You're starving. And you have no idea where you are. Every field looks the same. Every town looks the same. And straight ahead, there's nothing but sea. Your phone is at home, on your bed.
[hold 0.5]

### godwit
*A map of the Pacific: a line grows from ALASKA to TASMANIA. A small long-billed bird flies along it. 13,560 KM · 11 DAYS · NO STOPS.*
NARRATOR: Birds manage journeys much longer than yours. In twenty twenty two, a young bar tailed godwit, a bird that weighs less than a bag of sugar, flew from Alaska to Tasmania without stopping once. Thirteen and a half thousand kilometres, in eleven days, with no food and no rest. Nobody even taught it the way. It was making the trip for the first time. Birds find their way using the sun, the stars, and even the Earth's magnetic field. You've got none of that.
[hold 0.5]

### sleep
*Sunset over the sea. Your eyes droop. You drop. You jerk awake just above the waves. A frigatebird glides past with one eye shut. HALF ITS BRAIN ASLEEP · 2016.*
NARRATOR: You're so tired that you nod off, drop like a stone, and wake up with your toes in the sea. Some birds have solved this, too. In twenty sixteen, scientists found that frigatebirds can sleep while they fly, sometimes with just half of their brain at a time, keeping one eye open to see where they're going.
[pause 0.3]
NARRATOR: And common swifts can stay up in the air for ten months without ever landing.
[hold 0.5]

### tired
*You struggle above the waves, arms heavy, the coast far behind. Wind. Dark clouds on one side, sunny cliffs on the other.*
NARRATOR: But you can't sleep up here, and flying takes everything you've got. Your arms ache. The sun is going down. The coast is a thin line far behind you. You flap harder, and that only makes it worse. If you keep going like this, you won't make it.
[hold 0.5]

### thermal
*You remember the giant bird. Over the sunny cliffs, warm air rises in a twisting column. You spread your arms and circle up without effort. A condor circles beside you.*
NARRATOR: Then you remember the giant bird. It didn't flap. It soared. Over the sunny cliffs, warm air is rising like an invisible chimney. So you spread your arms, stop fighting, and let it carry you round and round, up and up, without doing anything at all.
[hold 0.4]

### condor
*An Andean condor with a tracker on its back. FLAPPING: 1% OF THE TIME. ONE BIRD: 172 KM WITHOUT A SINGLE FLAP. 2020.*
NARRATOR: Andean condors, the heaviest soaring birds alive today, live like this. In twenty twenty, scientists fitted them with trackers, and found they flap only about one percent of the time they're in the air. One flew for over five hours, and a hundred and seventy kilometres, without flapping once.
[hold 0.5]

### vee
*From the top of the thermal you see a flock of geese in a V, heading inland. You slot in at the end. Swirls of air come off each wingtip. You're carried.*
NARRATOR: From the top, you spot a flock of geese flying in a V, heading inland. Towards home. You tuck in at the end of the line, and suddenly flying gets easier. Every bird is riding the swirl of rising air that comes off the wingtip of the bird in front.
[pause 0.3]
NARRATOR: In twenty fourteen, scientists flew alongside a flock of ibises, and found the birds even time their wingbeats to catch that lift.
[hold 0.4]

### turns
*The lead goose drops back, tired. You fly up and take the front. Later, another goose takes over and you slide back. TAKING TURNS AT THE FRONT · 2015.*
NARRATOR: And the front of the V is the hardest place to be, because there's nobody to ride behind. So the birds take turns. When the leader gets tired, it drops back and another one takes over. So when it's your turn, you fly up to the front, and you lead the whole flock home.
[hold 0.5]

### home
*Dusk. Your street. The streetlights flick on. The geese honk and fly on. You land on your doorstep. Mum flings the door open: "Where have you BEEN?!" Then she hugs you.*
NARRATOR: The geese drop you off right over your street, just as the streetlights come on. You land on your doorstep, and your mum throws the door open. Where have you been, she says. And then she just hugs you, for a really long time.
[hold 0.5]

### night
*Bedroom at night. Window open, stars outside. You lie in bed, smiling. Far away, a V of geese crosses the moon.*
NARRATOR: That night, you lie in bed and think about it all. You flew higher than mountains and faster than cars, and you still nearly didn't make it home. Turns out even birds don't do the hard parts alone. They ride the warm air. They follow each other. And they take turns. Outside your window, far away, a flock of geese crosses the moon.
[hold 0.6]
