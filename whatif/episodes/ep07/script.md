# Episode 7: What If You Were Swallowed by a Whale?

One continuous story about YOU, present tense: on a dive trip you drop your friend's new underwater camera, dive after it, meet a blue whale that can't swallow you and a sperm whale that can, and end up inside its stomach. You survive on your scuba tank, find ambergris, make the whale throw you back up, and get home with a fortune that stinks. Real science all the way through: whale sizes and throats, echolocation clicks, stomach chambers and squid beaks, breath-holding and diving, ambergris, the whale pump, the 1891 hoax and the two real people who have been inside a whale's mouth. One narrator (Kokoro "Heart"). Speech appears on screen; nobody else speaks out loud.

How it's drawn: normal scale. A bright sea and a little dive boat, the blue underwater world with sunbeams and fish, then the inside of the whale: pink, ribbed, slimy walls lit only by your dive torch, a floor of black squid beaks, an acid pool. You wear a wetsuit, mask, scuba tank and fins.

Facts used, and where they come from:
- Blue whale: the largest animal known to have lived; up to about 30 m long (NOAA Fisheries); heart about 180 kg; tongue about 2.7 tonnes, roughly an Asian elephant (Guinness World Records, heaviest tongue).
- Baleen whales' throats are narrow: a blue whale's relaxed throat is under about 30 cm; a humpback can't swallow a person even though it can fit one in its mouth (National Geographic, "Most whales can't really swallow a human").
- Of about 90 kinds of whale, only the sperm whale has a throat big enough to swallow a person; it swallows squid whole (National Geographic).
- Echolocation: animals send out clicks and time the echoes; sound travels about 1,500 m/s in seawater versus about 343 m/s in air (over four times faster). Dolphins and bats echolocate too.
- Sperm whale clicks reach about 230 dB, the loudest sound of any animal; used for echolocation.
- Sperm whales have the largest brain of any animal, about 7.7 kg in mature males (NOAA via Massachusetts Division of Marine Fisheries); a human brain is about 1.3 kg. Teeth: 20 to 26 large teeth on each side of the lower jaw; upper teeth rarely break through the gums (NOAA Fisheries). The single blowhole is on the left side of the head, and the blow angles forward (NOAA Fisheries).
- Whale stomachs have several chambers; the first (forestomach) is a muscular chamber without digestive glands that crushes food; digestion with acid happens further on.
- Squid beaks resist digestion and build up in the stomach: whaling records report up to 14,000 beaks in one stomach (Discover magazine; New Zealand stranding study found 23,223 beaks in 16 stomachs).
- Whales are thought to throw up most squid beaks; ambergris forms when beaks pass into the gut and get coated in a waxy, fatty substance; it forms in roughly 1% of sperm whales (Whale and Dolphin Conservation; Smithsonian; Scientific American). Fresh ambergris is dark, soft and smells of faeces; it turns paler and sweet-smelling after years in the sea and sun, which is when it is most valuable.
- Yemen, 2021: 35 fishermen found about 127 kg of ambergris in a dead sperm whale off Aden and sold it for about $1.5 million (AFP / The National).
- Breath-holding: most people manage about a minute; Guinness record 24 min 37 s, Budimir Šobat (2021), after breathing pure oxygen. A standard scuba tank lasts roughly an hour near the surface.
- Sperm whales routinely dive 1,000 to 2,000 m and stay down more than an hour; deepest recorded about 2,250 m. Pressure rises about one atmosphere every 10 m. Their lungs collapse, and oxygen is stored in muscle myoglobin, so dense the meat is almost black.
- The whale pump: whales feed deep and defecate near the surface, fertilising phytoplankton, which produce around half the world's oxygen (IFAW; NOAA).
- James Bartley (1891), "a modern Jonah": historians found no James Bartley on the Star of the East's crew list and the ship was not a whaler; the captain's wife said there was "not one word of truth" in it.
- Michael Packard, lobster diver, Provincetown (Cape Cod), 11 June 2021: caught in a humpback's mouth about 45 ft down for about 30 seconds, kept breathing through his regulator, spat out at the surface; bruised, no broken bones (Center for Coastal Studies; AP).
- Adrián Simancas, kayaker, Strait of Magellan, Chile, 8 February 2025: briefly engulfed by a humpback with his yellow kayak and released within seconds; filmed by his father Dell; scientists say the whale was lunge-feeding and he was in the way (AP).

### blue
*A blue whale glides past, going on forever. Then the studio: the blue whale next to three buses; its heart on a scale, ABOUT 180 KG; its tongue on a scale next to an elephant.*
NARRATOR: It's a blue whale. The biggest animal known to have ever lived, bigger than any dinosaur. It can grow to around thirty metres long, about as long as three buses parked end to end. Its heart alone weighs about a hundred and eighty kilograms. And its tongue can weigh as much as an elephant.

### throat
*The whale opens its huge mouth and you brace yourself, but it's scooping up a cloud of tiny krill. A cutaway: its throat is only about as wide as a dinner plate. BALEEN WHALES CAN'T SWALLOW YOU.*
NARRATOR: It opens its mouth, and you think, this is it. But it's not after you. It's after a cloud of krill, tiny shrimp-like animals, millions of them. And here's the surprising part. For a whale that big, its throat is tiny. It's only about as wide as a dinner plate. A blue whale couldn't swallow you, even if it wanted to. Neither could a humpback.

### click

NARRATOR: But then, you hear something. Click. Click. Click. You can feel it in your chest. Rings of sound are bouncing off you, and something out there is listening to the echoes. Out of the dark comes a different whale, with a giant square head, and a thin lower jaw, full of teeth. A sperm whale.

### echo
*Studio: a sperm whale's head. A click ring leaves the front, travels to a squid, and bounces back. A timer: FAST ECHO = CLOSE · SLOW ECHO = FAR. Then a bat and a dolphin: SAME TRICK. SOUND IS OVER 4× FASTER IN WATER.*
NARRATOR: That clicking is called echolocation. The whale sends out a click, and listens for the echo. If the echo comes back fast, the thing is close. If it takes longer, it's further away. And sound travels more than four times faster in water than it does in air, so the echoes come back quickly. Dolphins use exactly the same trick. So do bats.
[hold 0.4]

### sperm
*Studio: a sperm whale turns slowly. Labels pop: LOUDEST ANIMAL ON EARTH · ABOUT 230 DECIBELS. BIGGEST BRAIN · ABOUT 8 KG, next to yours, 1.3 KG. TEETH ONLY IN THE LOWER JAW. Then a giant squid: SWALLOWED WHOLE.*
NARRATOR: Sperm whales are amazing. Their clicks are the loudest sound made by any animal, around two hundred and thirty decibels, and they use the echoes to find food in the pitch dark. They have the biggest brain of any animal on Earth, more than five times heavier than yours. Their big teeth are only in their lower jaw. And they hunt giant squid, and swallow them whole. Out of about ninety kinds of whale, the sperm whale is the only one with a throat big enough to swallow a person.

### gulp

NARRATOR: Now, in real life, a sperm whale would almost certainly never do this. You are not a squid. But this whale is having a very strange day. It swings round, drops open its jaw, and sucks you in, along with your friend's camera. Snap. Darkness. And down the slimy tunnel you go.

### beaks
*Back in the first chamber, torch on. The walls squeeze and ripple. The floor: thousands of black squid beaks. Your camera is lying among them. UP TO 14,000 SQUID BEAKS IN ONE STOMACH.*
NARRATOR: Which brings us back to now. A sperm whale's stomach has several chambers, and you've landed in the first one. There's no acid in here. It's a big squeezing bag of muscle, that crushes and churns whatever comes in. And the floor is covered in squid beaks. Squid have hard, sharp beaks that a whale can't digest, and whalers found up to fourteen thousand of them in a single stomach. Oh, and there's your camera.
[hold 0.4]

### air
*A gauge on your wrist: AIR. Science: a stopwatch. MOST PEOPLE · ABOUT 1 MINUTE. WORLD RECORD · 24 MIN 37 S, AFTER BREATHING PURE OXYGEN. Then your scuba tank, a bar draining: ABOUT AN HOUR.*
NARRATOR: The next problem is air. There isn't any. A whale's stomach has no oxygen in it at all. Most people can hold their breath for about a minute. The world record is over twenty four minutes, but that was after breathing pure oxygen first. Luckily, you've got a scuba tank. Near the surface, a tank like yours lasts about an hour. So, you've got one hour to get out of a whale.
[hold 0.4]

### dive
*The floor tilts. Everything slides. The whale is diving. A depth meter spins: 100 M, 500 M, 1,000 M. Then the studio: the whale diving into the deep: 1–2 KM DOWN · OVER AN HOUR. Its lungs fold flat; its muscles glow dark red: OXYGEN STORED IN MUSCLE · MYOGLOBIN.*
NARRATOR: Then the whole floor tips forward. Uh oh. The whale is diving. Sperm whales often dive one or two kilometres down, and stay there for over an hour. The deepest one ever measured went about two and a quarter kilometres. Down there, the water presses with over two hundred times the pressure you feel at the surface. Luckily for you, this whale has just eaten. It turns back up.
[pause 0.3]
NARRATOR: So how does a whale survive down there? Its lungs fold flat, and it carries its oxygen in its muscles instead, packed into a protein called myoglobin. There's so much of it, that sperm whale meat is almost black.
[hold 0.4]

### acid
*You squeeze through a tight ring of muscle into the next chamber. A pool of sizzling yellow-green liquid. Your gloves start to fizz. CHAMBER 2 · ACID.*
NARRATOR: You squeeze through a tight ring of muscle into the next chamber, and it's even worse. This is where the acid is. Your gloves start to fizz. In cartoons, stomach acid melts things in seconds. In real life it's slower, and it takes hours to digest a meal. But your skin would start to sting and burn, and you do not want to stay for those hours.
[hold 0.4]

### grey
*Deeper in, wedged in the wall: a lumpy grey waxy blob the size of a football. You poke it. AMBERGRIS. Studio: a squid beak getting wrapped in wax, layer by layer. FOUND IN ABOUT 1 IN 100 SPERM WHALES. A perfume bottle. A fishing boat with a huge lump: YEMEN · 2021 · 127 KG · ABOUT $1.5 MILLION.*
NARRATOR: Deeper in, your torch lands on something odd. A big, grey, waxy lump. You've just found ambergris. When a squid beak slips further down the whale's gut, the whale coats it in a waxy, fatty stuff, layer after layer, until it becomes a lump like this. Only about one in a hundred sperm whales ever makes it. And it's used in some of the most expensive perfumes in the world. In twenty twenty one, fishermen in Yemen found a hundred and twenty seven kilograms of it inside a dead sperm whale, and sold it for about one and a half million dollars.
[hold 0.4]

### pump
*Studio: a whale dives deep, then surfaces and lets out a big brown cloud. Tiny green phytoplankton bloom in it, giving off bubbles: O₂. THE WHALE PUMP. PHYTOPLANKTON MAKE ABOUT HALF THE WORLD'S OXYGEN.*
NARRATOR: And whales do more than make perfume. They feed deep down, and then poop near the surface, bringing nutrients up with them. That feeds tiny ocean plants called phytoplankton. And phytoplankton make around half of the oxygen on our planet. Scientists call it the whale pump. So in a way, whales help you breathe.
[hold 0.4]

### myth
*An old newspaper: 1891 · A MODERN JONAH. A sailor drawing. The ship's crew list, no Bartley on it. A red stamp: HOAX.*
NARRATOR: So has anyone ever really been swallowed by a whale? In eighteen ninety one, a famous story said a sailor called James Bartley was swallowed by a sperm whale, and found alive inside it the next day. But when historians checked, there was no James Bartley on the ship's crew list. The captain's wife said there wasn't one word of truth in it. It was a hoax.
[hold 0.4]

### real
*A lobster diver near the sea floor; a humpback lunges and he disappears into its mouth: 2021 · CAPE COD · ABOUT 30 SECONDS. It surfaces and spits him out. Then a yellow kayak in Patagonia: 2025 · CHILE · A COUPLE OF SECONDS.*
NARRATOR: But two people really have ended up inside a whale's mouth. In twenty twenty one, a lobster diver called Michael Packard was scooped up by a humpback off Cape Cod. He spent about thirty seconds in the dark, still breathing through his scuba gear, until the whale surfaced and spat him out. He was bruised, but nothing was broken. And in twenty twenty five, a humpback in Chile gulped up a kayaker called Adrián Simancas, and let him go a couple of seconds later. His dad filmed the whole thing. Both times, the whale wasn't hunting anyone. They were just in the way of its lunch.

### twist
