If he pulls out your birthday, you could be going to war.

This wasn't a film. It was real. And nearly forty years later, a writer whose own father had been sent to that war would give the world a story about a lottery that sends children to fight.

Let's start with the fiction. Every year in Panem, every child aged twelve to eighteen has their name entered into a glass bowl. One boy and one girl from each of the twelve districts are drawn. Twenty-four tributes. One survivor.

In Sunrise on the Reaping, it's even worse. It's the fiftieth Hunger Games, a Quarter Quell, and the Capitol doubles the number of tributes. Forty-eight children. And one of them is a sixteen-year-old called Haymitch Abernathy.

But here's the detail most people forget. The reaping isn't fair. If your family is starving, you can sign up for extra food, called tesserae. The catch? Every tessera puts your name in the bowl one more time.

So the poorest kids have the worst odds. Remember that. It's going to matter.

First, where did the idea come from? Suzanne Collins has told the story herself. One night, she was flicking between channels. On one, a reality TV show, with young people competing for a prize. On the other, real footage of the war in Iraq.

And she says the two started to blur together.

There's an older inspiration too. Collins has said that as a child she loved the Greek myth of Theseus. In the myth, Athens is punished by being forced to send seven young men and seven young women to Crete, to be thrown into a labyrinth with the Minotaur.

Young people, chosen as tribute, sent to die, to remind a city who's in charge. Sound familiar?

But that's a myth. What we're looking for is evidence. A time when a real government really did pick young people's fates out of a container, live, in front of the whole country.

And that brings us back to 1969.

The United States is deep in the Vietnam War. The army needs soldiers, and the old way of choosing who gets drafted is seen as unfair. So the government tries something new. A lottery.

On the evening of December the first, 1969, at the headquarters of the Selective Service in Washington, they place three hundred and sixty-six blue capsules in a container. One for every possible birthday, including the twenty-ninth of February.

It's for men born between 1944 and 1950. And the whole thing is broadcast live on television.

A congressman named Alexander Pirnie reaches in and pulls out the first capsule. The date inside: September the fourteenth.

If you were born on September the fourteenth, you were now number one. First in line.

One by one, the dates are pulled out and pinned to a board. Across America, families sit in their living rooms, waiting to hear a birthday. Their son's birthday. Some people cheered. Some people cried.

In the end, every man whose birthday came out in the first one hundred and ninety-five was called up for possible service in 1970.

Now, to be clear, Suzanne Collins has never said the draft lottery inspired the reaping. But look at the parallels. Young people. Chosen by chance. Their names, or their birthdays, drawn from a container. Broadcast to the nation. While their families watch, and wait.

And remember what I said about the reaping not being fair? Here's the twist.

The 1969 lottery wasn't fair either.

The capsules had been put into the container month by month, starting with January. And they weren't mixed properly. December's capsules went in last, and ended up near the top.

The result? If you were born late in the year, you were more likely to be drawn early. Statisticians spotted the pattern almost straight away. The government insisted the lottery was fair. A court later said it was perhaps not scientifically perfect, but fair. And the next year, they changed the system.

In Panem, being poor puts your name in the bowl more times. In 1969, just being born in December stacked the odds against you.

Here's where the story becomes personal.

Suzanne Collins's father was an officer in the United States Air Force, and a military historian. During the Vietnam War, he was sent to Vietnam for a year. Suzanne was six years old.

She later wrote a picture book about that year, called Year of the Jungle. A little girl, waiting for her dad to come home from a war she doesn't understand.

Collins has said her father made sure his children understood war. He took the family to battlefields and talked to them about it. And she has said that war is at the heart of The Hunger Games.

So the author of the most famous story about children sent to fight grew up as a child waiting for her own father to come home from one.

There's one more clue, and it's hidden in the name of the country itself.

Panem. In Mockingjay, it's explained that the name comes from an old Latin phrase. Panem et circenses. Bread and circuses.

It was written nearly two thousand years ago by a Roman poet called Juvenal. He complained that the people of Rome had given up their power, and now only cared about two things. Free bread, and the games.

Rome handed out free grain, and filled the arenas with gladiators, chariot races and blood. Keep the crowd fed. Keep the crowd entertained. And they won't ask questions.

That's the Capitol's whole plan.

Which brings us to Sunrise on the Reaping, and that philosopher.

When Suzanne Collins announced the book, she said it was inspired by David Hume, a Scottish philosopher from the seventeen hundreds. And one idea in particular. What he called "the easiness with which the many are governed by the few."

Hume's question was simple. Why do huge numbers of people let a small group control them? His answer was opinion. Rulers only stay in power as long as people believe they should.

Collins calls it implicit submission. And she says it depends on propaganda. On controlling the story people are told. That's why Haymitch's story was the one she wanted to tell. A boy who sees the truth behind the Games, in a country that will do anything to hide it.

So, let's look at the evidence.

A real government did draw young people's birthdays from a container, live on television, to decide who would be sent to war. That lottery really was unfair. The author's own father really did serve in that war. The name Panem really does come from Rome's bread and circuses. And the newest book really is built on a philosopher's question about why the many obey the few.

There's no real arena. No real tributes fighting to the death on live TV. But almost every other piece of The Hunger Games has a real-world shadow.

So, how real is the reaping? The evidence says… eight out of ten.
