This case has three questions. One. Would Dexter's science even get into a courtroom? Two. What happens when the man in the lab is the criminal? And three. How long would he really survive?

First, the fiction. Dexter began as a novel, Jeff Lindsay's Darkly Dreaming Dexter, published in 2004. Two years later, Showtime turned it into a series, with Michael C. Hall as Dexter. It ran for eight seasons.

Dexter is a blood spatter analyst for the Miami Metro Police Department. He reads the drops, the smears and the angles at a crime scene, and he tells detectives exactly what happened. He's never wrong.

And the whole show rests on one idea. The man the system trusts most is the one it should trust least.

Exhibit A. The gatekeeper.

In a real trial, an expert can't just walk in and give an opinion. First, a judge decides whether the science is good enough for a jury to hear. And in America, there are two main tests.

The older one comes from a 1923 case about an early lie detector. It's called the Frye test, and it asks one question. Is this method generally accepted by the experts in its field?

The newer one comes from a 1993 Supreme Court case called Daubert. It turns the judge into a gatekeeper. Has the method been tested? Has it been peer reviewed? And what is its error rate?

Now here's the twist. Dexter works in Florida. And Florida couldn't make up its mind. In 2013, the state legislature switched to Daubert. In 2017, the Florida Supreme Court refused to go along with it. In 2018, it said no again. And then, in May 2019, it changed its mind and adopted Daubert after all.

Which means that for almost the whole original run of the show, Florida's courts were really using the older test. And under that test, a blood spatter expert only had to convince... other blood spatter experts.

So, Exhibit A. Dexter's testimony walks straight in. But getting into court is one thing. Holding up is another.

Exhibit B. The science on the stand.

Blood spatter analysis is real. In 1971, a criminalist called Herbert MacDonell published a study for the US Justice Department called Flight Characteristics and Stain Patterns of Human Blood. He'd worked it out in his own lab, in Corning, New York. And soon, police across America were learning to read blood.

By the time Dexter came along, it was a regular part of real murder trials. Then, in 2009, the National Academy of Sciences took a hard look at it. And its verdict was blunt. The uncertainties associated with bloodstain pattern analysis are enormous. In general, it said, the opinions of bloodstain pattern analysts are more subjective than scientific.

In 2021, a study funded by the US National Institute of Justice put that to the test. Seventy five working analysts examined one hundred and ninety two bloodstain patterns where the real cause was already known. About one answer in every nine was wrong.

One in nine. And when that goes wrong in a courtroom, it can cost a man his life.

In 1985, in the small town of Clifton, Texas, a teacher named Mickey Bryan was killed in her home. Her husband, Joe, a school principal, was at a conference in another city. But a police detective told the jury that blood on a flashlight in Joe's car put him at the scene. That detective had forty hours of training in blood spatter.

Joe Bryan was convicted. More than thirty years later, Texas's Forensic Science Commission found that the blood spatter evidence was not accurate or scientifically supported. The expert himself admitted, my conclusions were wrong. Joe Bryan finally left prison on parole in March 2020. He was seventy nine. He'd spent thirty three years inside, and his conviction still stands.

Now, to be fair to Dexter. He isn't a detective with a short course. He's a full time lab analyst. He'd be better trained than the expert who convicted Joe Bryan. But a former head of the San Diego police crime lab looked at what Dexter does on screen, pointing to one exact spot, and telling one exact story, and said it's most definitely made for TV.

So, Exhibit B. The science is real. But it's nowhere near as certain as Dexter makes it look. A good defence lawyer would take him apart on the error rate alone.

Exhibit C. When the analyst is the criminal.

But Dexter's biggest problem in court isn't the science. It's Dexter. Because the man explaining the evidence is also the man hiding it, taking it and changing it. And in real life, we know exactly what happens when a crime lab insider gets caught.

In 2012, investigators found that a chemist named Annie Dookhan, at a state drug lab in Boston, had been faking her work. She'd reported positive results for drugs she never actually tested, and she'd forged her colleagues' signatures.

When it came out, every case she had ever touched was suddenly in doubt. And in April 2017, Massachusetts threw out twenty one thousand, five hundred and eighty seven of them, all at once. Campaigners called it the largest dismissal of wrongful convictions in American history.

Then it happened again. Sonja Farak, a chemist at another state lab in Amherst, had been secretly using the drugs she was meant to be testing. In the end, more than twenty four thousand drug charges were dismissed.

And the courts made the rules very clear. Prosecutors must hand the defence anything that damages their own witnesses. And since 2009, the Supreme Court has said a lab report can't just be read out in court. The analyst who wrote it can be called to the stand and cross examined. Right in the middle of Dexter's run.

So picture the real version. The day Dexter is exposed, it isn't just one case that falls apart. Every bloodstain report he ever signed, across years of Miami murders, goes back under the microscope. Killers he helped convict could walk free. The man who thought he was cleaning up the system would become its biggest mess.

And for the record, we found no scandal like this at Miami Dade's real crime lab. That part is pure fiction.

So, Exhibit C. Dexter's evidence might survive a courtroom. Dexter wouldn't.

Exhibit D. The clock.

So, how long would Dexter really last? Let's start with the good news, for him. Back in 1965, police in America solved about nine in every ten murders. By 2021, it had fallen to less than half.

And Miami is one of the hardest places to get caught. In Miami Dade, the share of murders solved fell from sixty four percent in 2013, to just thirty six percent in 2017.

So for one crime, the odds are close to a coin flip. But Dexter isn't committing one crime. And every time you flip that coin again, the chance of never losing gets smaller. Flip it ten times, and the chance of winning every single time is less than one in a thousand.

Of course, real cases aren't coin flips. Killers slip through when police forces don't connect the dots. Criminologists even have a name for it. Linkage blindness. Dexter depends on it.

But here's what real cases show. Long running killers are rarely caught by a brilliant detective with a hunch, like Sergeant Doakes. They're caught by one piece of data.

In April 2018, investigators caught the Golden State Killer, decades after his crimes, by uploading crime scene DNA to a public genealogy website, and building his family tree.

Gary Ridgway, the Green River Killer, was caught in 2001, when new DNA testing matched a sample police had taken from him back in 1987.

Israel Keyes was a careful killer who travelled across the country. He was caught within weeks of his last crime, because of a bank card.

And in Gary, Indiana, a computer program written by a journalist flagged a cluster of unsolved killings in 2010. Four years before police caught the man responsible.

Now picture Miami today. DNA from a crime scene can be checked against family trees. By the end of 2023, genetic genealogy had helped solve more than six hundred and fifty criminal cases. One company alone says its cameras scan twenty billion licence plates a month. Dexter's whole routine was built for a world before any of this.

And what about real people who did what Dexter does, and went after criminals? In Washington State, there are two documented cases. Patrick Drum, in 2012, was sentenced to life without parole within three months. Michael Mullen, in 2005, died in prison less than two years later. Neither of them came close to lasting as long as Dexter.

So how long would Dexter survive? Not decades. In my judgement, one slip, one DNA match, one bank record, one pattern a computer spots, and it's over. Months. A few years at most. And in 2026, probably less.
