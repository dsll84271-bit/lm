## 1. 2009 NAS report on bloodstain pattern analysis (BPA): CONFIRMED (fix the page cite)
- Exact wording (two separate sentences on facing pages):
  - p. 178: "In general, the opinions of bloodstain pattern analysts are more subjective than scientific."
  - p. 179: "The uncertainties associated with bloodstain pattern analysis are enormous."
- Page numbers: in the final NAP edition (the NCJRS copy) these are **pp. 178–179**, not 5-39. Cite "pp. 178–179" unless you are quoting the prepublication draft.

- Source: https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/grants/228091.pdf (NAP catalog 12589)

## 2. NIJ-funded BPA black-box study (Hicklin et al.): CONFIRMED
- 75 analysts, 192 patterns, 11.2% erroneous, 7.8% contradicted. Each analyst got 150 of the 192 samples, and the study collected 33,005 responses.
- Abstract quote: "We investigated conclusions made by 75 practicing bloodstain pattern analysts on 192 bloodstain patterns ... On prompts for which the cause of the depicted bloodstain was known, 11.2% of responses were erroneous ... 7.8% of responses contradicted other analysts."
- The NIJ article rounds these figures ("11% of the time"; "about 8%"). The article is dated 14 December 2022. The underlying paper is *Forensic Science International* 325 (2021) 110856, and the NIJ grantee report is dated March 2021.
- Precision note: 11.2% is the share of *responses* that were erroneous, not the share of analysts or cases.
- Sources: https://nij.ojp.gov/topics/articles/study-assesses-accuracy-and-reproducibility-bloodstain-pattern-analysis ; https://www.ojp.gov/pdffiles1/nij/grants/304645.pdf

## 4. TFSC "requiring accreditation" for BPA: CORRECTED
- Texas did **not** end up requiring accreditation for BPA. In 2018 the TFSC moved toward requiring it, and ProPublica reported in Dec 2018 that the commission was "stipulating that such analysis must be performed by an accredited organization". It then reversed course:
  - FY2019 annual report: "Effective December 16, 2018, Commissioners exempted the discipline from accreditation requirements."
  - On 15 July 2019 the working group "changed its recommended oversight approach from accreditation to voluntary licensure." On 16 August 2019 "the Commission approved the approach of voluntary licensure for crime scene reconstructionists."
  - The TFSC now offers a **voluntary** license: "Crime Scene Reconstruction Analyst (Bloodstain Pattern)."
- Suggested wording: "After the Bryan case, Texas's Forensic Science Commission considered requiring accreditation for blood-spatter analysis. In 2019 it settled on a voluntary licence instead."
- Sources: https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1445412/fsc-annual-report-fy2019-pdf.pdf ; https://www.txcourts.gov/media/1462101/fsc-annual-report-fy2018.pdf ; https://www.txcourts.gov/fsc/licensing/voluntary-licensure/

## 5. Florida Frye/Daubert timeline: CONFIRMED
Primary court and legislature sites were blocked, so this rests on Wikipedia plus consistent search snippets of the opinions and bill pages.
- 2013: HB 7015, chapter 2013-107, Laws of Florida, amended §§ 90.702 and 90.704. The governor signed it in June 2013 and it took effect on **1 July 2013**. Wikipedia: "Florida passed a bill to adopt the Daubert standard ... which took effect on July 1, 2013."
- 16 Feb 2017: *In re Amendments to the Florida Evidence Code*, 210 So. 3d 1231. The court declined to adopt the Daubert amendments as a rule of procedure.
- 15 Oct 2018: *DeLisle v. Crane Co.*, 258 So. 3d 1219 (No. SC16-2182). The court held the 2013 amendment unconstitutional, as an intrusion on its rulemaking power, and kept Frye.
- 23 May 2019: *In re Amendments to the Florida Evidence Code*, SC19-107, 278 So. 3d 551. The court adopted ch. 2013-107 §§ 1–2, effective immediately. Wikipedia: "On May 23, 2019, the Florida Supreme Court accepted the Daubert standard."
- Sources: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daubert_standard ; https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2013/7015 (snippet) ; https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/2019/sc19-107.html (snippet)

## 6. FRE 702 amendment, 1 Dec 2023: CONFIRMED
- Current rule text: "...may testify in the form of an opinion or otherwise if the proponent demonstrates to the court that it is more likely than not that: ..."
- 2023 committee note: "expert testimony may not be admitted unless the proponent demonstrates to the court that it is more likely than not that the proffered testimony meets the admissibility requirements."
- Effective date: **1 December 2023**. Cornell's statutory-history line has not been updated to show this, but the date is consistent across sources.
- Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/rules/fre/rule_702

## 7. Annie Dookhan: CONFIRMED

## 9. *Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts* (2009): CONFIRMED
- Decided 25 June 2009 (5–4). The court held that analysts' certificates are testimonial, so the analysts must be available for cross-examination.
- Quotes: "In short, under our decision in Crawford the analysts' affidavits were testimonial statements, and the analysts were 'witnesses' for purposes of the Sixth Amendment." / "Absent a showing that the analysts were unavailable to testify at trial and that petitioner had a prior opportunity to cross-examine them, petitioner was entitled to 'be confronted with' the analysts at trial."
- Source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/07-591.ZO.html

## 10. Murder clearance rates: CORRECTED (2020 figure; 2025 data now exists)
- 1965: "about 90%" holds. Sources say "over 90 percent" in the mid-1960s. No exact FBI figure was reachable because fbi.gov was blocked.
- 2020: the FBI estimate was about **54%** (Marshall Project, from search snippets: "only 54 percent of homicides were cleared"). "About half" is fine, but "about 50%" understates it. The record low was **49.4% in 2021**, a year when FBI data were incomplete because of the switch to the new NIBRS reporting system.
- 2024: **61.4%** CONFIRMED (CAP: "the clearance rate for murder increased to 61.4 in 2024").
- **New:** FBI 2025 data released in August 2026 put murder clearance at **68.8%**, the highest since 1988 (Jeff Asher and the Murder Accountability Project, from search snippets). If the script says "latest", it should use 2025 / 68.8% or say "61.4% in 2024".
- Miami-Dade: "64.3 percent in 2013; by 2017, it had dropped to 36 percent". This matches Miami New Times (seen as a search snippet; the page was blocked) and refers to Miami-Dade County.
- Sources: https://americanprogress.org/article/nationwide-2024-crime-data-demonstrate-the-value-of-violence-prevention-and-local-law-enforcement/ ; https://www.themarshallproject.org/2022/01/12/as-murders-spiked-police-solved-about-half-in-2020 ; https://jasher.substack.com/p/murders-in-2025-were-likely-solved ; https://miaminewtimes.com/news/miami-dade-county-is-worst-in-florida-at-solving-crime-10939171
