The state in Grand Theft Auto Six doesn't exist. It's called Leonida. It has a neon city, a swamp full of alligators, a chain of tropical islands, and some of the strangest people in gaming.

But almost none of it was invented. Every piece of Leonida, from the pink hotels to the crazy headlines, was taken from one real place.

And the real story is wilder than the game. It's a city that was started by a frost, sold off by the square foot, flattened by a hurricane, painted pink to save it, and is now in a race against the sea.

So, why Florida? Why does Rockstar keep coming back here? To answer that, we have to go back more than a hundred and thirty years, to a winter when Florida froze.

In the winter of 1894, a series of hard freezes swept across Florida. Over a few weeks, the orange groves in the north of the state were wiped out. Growers lost almost everything.

But hundreds of miles to the south, on the shore of Biscayne Bay, the trees survived. A landowner there named Julia Tuttle had been trying to convince a railroad tycoon, Henry Flagler, to bring his trains down to her empty corner of the map.

After the freeze, Flagler sent a man south to take a look. The legend says he came back carrying fresh orange blossoms. Historians think it may have been a mixed bunch of flowers. Either way, the message landed. Down there, nothing froze.

Flagler's railroad arrived in the spring of 1896. That summer, on July the twenty-eighth, a few hundred voters met and made it a city. They called it Miami.

That's the first thing Leonida borrowed from Florida. This was never an old place that grew slowly. It was invented, fast, by people selling a dream. And the dream was about to get out of control.

By the 1920s, everyone wanted a piece of Florida. Swamps were drained. Islands were dredged up out of the bay. Land sold so fast that people bought plots they had never seen, just to sell them on a few weeks later.

A man named Carl Fisher turned a mangrove swamp into a resort town called Miami Beach. George Merrick built a whole Spanish-style city called Coral Gables. Fortunes were made in months.

Then, on the eighteenth of September, 1926, the eye of a major hurricane passed straight over Miami.

Many people had never seen a hurricane before. When the calm eye arrived, they walked outside, thinking the storm was over. Then the other side hit.

At least three hundred and seventy-two people died. Whole neighbourhoods were flattened. And the boom, which was already starting to crack, collapsed. Land that had sold for a fortune was suddenly almost worthless.

Remember that, because it's the pattern of this whole story. Florida booms. Florida busts. And then Florida reinvents itself. Which brings us to the colours.

In the 1930s and 40s, Miami Beach rebuilt with hundreds of small hotels in a sleek new style. Rounded corners. Racing stripes. Glass blocks. Neon signs. Today, we call it Art Deco.

But by the 1970s, those hotels were tired and run-down, and developers wanted to knock them down and build towers.

A woman named Barbara Capitman refused to let that happen. She and her friends formed the Miami Design Preservation League, and in May 1979 they got more than eight hundred buildings listed as a national historic district. It was the first district of twentieth-century buildings in America to get that recognition.

Then her co-founder, a designer named Leonard Horowitz, did something clever. He repainted the faded buildings in soft pinks, mint greens and lavenders, to make people stop and actually look at them.

So here's the twist. The candy-coloured Miami that Vice City is famous for? A lot of it is a paint job from the 1980s.

And that same decade was about to give Miami a whole new reputation. But first, we need to leave the city, and head west, into the swamp.

Drive west out of Miami and the city stops almost instantly. In its place is one of the strangest landscapes on Earth. The Everglades.

It looks like a giant field of grass. It's actually a river. A slow, shallow sheet of water, flowing south toward the sea, often only a few inches to a few feet deep, and in places dozens of miles wide.

In 1947, a writer named Marjory Stoneman Douglas gave it the name people still use today. The River of Grass. Her book came out in November. A month later, President Truman dedicated Everglades National Park.

And it is full of alligators. Florida's wildlife agency estimates there are about one point three million of them in the state.

In the Grand Theft Auto Six trailers, this part of Leonida is called Grassrivers. Flip that around, and it's pretty much Douglas's River of Grass.

But the swamp has a newer monster. Burmese pythons, most likely escaped or released pets, now live wild in the Everglades. Some grow longer than a car. Contractors working with the state have removed more than nineteen thousand of them since 2017. And they're still there.

So. A neon city on one side. A prehistoric swamp on the other. That contrast is Leonida in a nutshell. But there's one more thing Florida is famous for, and it isn't a place. It's a person.

Florida Man. Florida Man arrested for... well, you've seen the headlines. A whole internet joke is built on the idea that Florida produces the strangest people in America.

But there's a much more boring explanation. Florida has one of the strongest public records laws in the country. Arrest reports and police mugshots are open to almost anyone who asks.

So a reporter can find a strange arrest in minutes, and write about it the same day. In many other states, those records are much harder to get.

Some experts think the law isn't the whole story. But it does mean that Florida doesn't necessarily have weirder people. We just get to read about them.

And Rockstar clearly loves this. The Grand Theft Auto Six trailers are packed with viral-video chaos that feels ripped straight from real Florida headlines.

Now, back to Miami, and the decade that gave Vice City its look.

In 1980, over about six months, roughly a hundred and twenty-five thousand people crossed from Cuba to South Florida on a fleet of boats. It was called the Mariel boatlift, and it changed Miami almost overnight.

At the same time, South Florida had become a major gateway for cocaine coming into the United States. Drug money poured into the city. And so did violence.

In 1981, Time magazine put Miami on its cover under the headline: Paradise Lost?

The new arrivals got much of the blame. But one criminologist later estimated they accounted for only about a quarter of the rise in crime.

Hollywood saw a story. In 1983, Scarface. In 1984, a TV show called Miami Vice. Pastel suits, fast cars, neon, and synth music.

In 2002, Rockstar took that exact mix, the pastel hotels, the cocaine-era crime, the Miami Vice soundtrack, and turned it into Grand Theft Auto: Vice City.

So when Grand Theft Auto Six goes back to Vice City, it isn't really going back to Miami. It's going back to a memory of Miami. One built from movies, TV shows, and a paint job from the 1980s.

Here's what the game can't show you. In 1900, about half a million people lived in the whole state of Florida. By 2020, it was twenty-one and a half million.

That's more than forty times bigger. In 2014, Florida overtook New York to become the third biggest state in America by population.

Every one of those people needed somewhere to live. So Florida kept doing what it did in the 1920s. Draining. Dredging. Building. Right up to the edge of the water.

And that edge is moving.

Miami sits on limestone, a rock so full of holes that it works like a sponge. When the sea rises, the water doesn't just come over the top. It comes up from underneath.

That's why some streets in Miami flood on perfectly sunny days, during the highest tides of the year. The king tides.

Local planners expect about two feet of sea level rise by around 2060. And NOAA has warned that this kind of high-tide flooding could hit Miami as often as fifty days a year by 2050.

Miami Beach is fighting back. It's raising roads, and spending hundreds of millions of dollars on pumps to push the water back out to sea.

It might work for decades. Nobody knows if it works for a century.

So, why Florida? Because no other place packs this much into one story. A city started by a frost. A boom wiped out by a hurricane. A neighbourhood saved with paint. A river made of grass. A crime wave turned into a TV show. And headlines that never stop.

Rockstar can rebuild Vice City whenever it wants. The real one is in a race against the sea.
