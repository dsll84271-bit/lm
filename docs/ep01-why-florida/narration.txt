The state in Grand Theft Auto Six doesn't exist. It's called Leonida. It has a neon city, a swamp full of alligators, a chain of tropical islands, and some of the strangest people in gaming.

But almost none of it was invented. Every piece of Leonida was taken from one real place. And the real story is wilder than the game.

By the end of this video, you'll know why the hotels in Vice City are pink, where the name Grassrivers really comes from, why Florida Man exists, and why the real Vice City is in a race it might not win.

So, why Florida? Let's start with the city at the heart of it all.

In the game, Vice City feels like it has always been there. But the real Miami is younger than the Eiffel Tower.

In the winter of 1894, a series of hard freezes wiped out the orange groves across northern Florida. But hundreds of miles to the south, on Biscayne Bay, the trees survived.

A landowner there, Julia Tuttle, had been begging a railroad tycoon named Henry Flagler to bring his trains down to her empty corner of the map. After the freeze, he finally listened. The legend says she proved her point with a box of fresh orange blossoms.

The railroad arrived in 1896. That summer, a few hundred voters made it a city. They called it Miami.

So Vice City's real twin wasn't an old city that grew slowly. It was invented, fast, by people selling a dream. And that dream was about to spin out of control.

Leonida is a state built on hustle. Get rich quick, cash out, lose it all. That attitude is a hundred years old.

In the 1920s, everyone wanted a piece of Florida. Swamps were drained. Islands were dredged up out of the bay. People bought land they had never even seen, just to sell it on a few weeks later. A man named Carl Fisher turned a mangrove swamp into a resort called Miami Beach. Fortunes were made in months.

Then, on the eighteenth of September, 1926, the eye of a major hurricane passed straight over Miami.

Many people had never seen a hurricane. When the calm eye arrived, they walked outside, thinking the storm was over. Then the other side hit.

At least three hundred and seventy-two people died. Whole neighbourhoods were flattened. The boom collapsed, and land that had sold for a fortune was suddenly almost worthless.

Boom. Bust. Reinvent. Remember that pattern, because it explains everything that comes next. And stick around, because the most famous thing about Vice City didn't come from Miami at all. It came from a TV show.

In Vice City, the beachfront is lined with pastel hotels. Pink, mint, lavender. It's the most famous look in gaming. So where did it come from?

In the 1930s, Miami Beach rebuilt with hundreds of small hotels in a sleek new style. Rounded corners. Racing stripes. Glass blocks. Neon signs. Art Deco.

But by the 1970s, those hotels were run-down, and developers wanted to bulldoze them for towers.

A woman named Barbara Capitman fought back. In 1979, her group got more than eight hundred of those buildings protected as a national historic district, the first of its kind for twentieth-century buildings in America.

Then her co-founder, a designer named Leonard Horowitz, did something clever. He repainted the faded buildings in soft pinks, mint greens and lavenders, so people would finally stop and look at them.

So here's the twist. The candy-coloured Miami that Vice City is famous for? A lot of it is a paint job from the 1980s.

Now leave the city. In the trailers, there's a huge swamp called Grassrivers. It's based on a real place, the Everglades.

It looks like a giant field of grass. It's actually a river. A slow, shallow sheet of water flowing south toward the sea, often only a few inches deep, and in places dozens of miles wide.

And it's full of alligators. Florida's wildlife agency estimates there are about one point three million of them in the state.

But the swamp has a newer monster. Burmese pythons, most likely escaped or released pets, now live wild in the Everglades. Some grow longer than a car. Contractors working with the state have removed more than nineteen thousand of them since 2017. And they're still there.

So why Grassrivers? In 1947, a writer named Marjory Stoneman Douglas gave the Everglades the name people still use today. The River of Grass. A month after her book came out, Everglades National Park was officially dedicated.

River of Grass. Grassrivers. Rockstar just flipped it around.

The Grand Theft Auto Six trailers are packed with chaos. People filming each other doing insane things, like it's straight off a Florida news feed. That's not an accident. It's a joke the whole internet already knows. Florida Man.

Florida Man arrested for... well, you've seen the headlines. The joke is that Florida produces the strangest people in America.

But there's a much more boring explanation. Florida has one of the strongest public records laws in the country. Arrest reports and police mugshots are open to almost anyone who asks. So reporters can find a strange arrest in minutes, and publish it the same day.

Some experts think the law isn't the whole story. But it means Florida doesn't necessarily have weirder people. We just get to read about them.

Now, that TV show. To understand where Vice City really comes from, we have to go back to 1980.

That year, over about six months, roughly a hundred and twenty-five thousand people crossed from Cuba to South Florida on a fleet of boats. It was called the Mariel boatlift.

At the same time, South Florida had become a major gateway for cocaine coming into the United States. Drug money poured into Miami. And so did violence. In 1981, Time magazine put the city on its cover under the headline: Paradise Lost?

The new arrivals got much of the blame. But one criminologist later estimated they accounted for only about a quarter of the rise in crime.

Hollywood saw a story. In 1983, Scarface. In 1984, a TV show called Miami Vice. Pastel suits, fast cars, neon, and synth music.

And in 2002, Rockstar took that exact mix, the pastel hotels, the cocaine-era crime, the Miami Vice soundtrack, and turned it into Grand Theft Auto: Vice City.

So when Grand Theft Auto Six goes back to Vice City, it isn't really going back to Miami. It's going back to a memory of Miami. One built from movies, TV shows, and a paint job from the 1980s.

But the real Miami kept growing. In 1900, about half a million people lived in the whole state of Florida. By 2020, it was twenty-one and a half million. More than forty times bigger. In 2014, Florida overtook New York to become the third biggest state in America by population.

And all those people needed somewhere to live. So Florida kept doing what it did in the 1920s. Draining. Dredging. Building. Right up to the edge of the water.

And that edge is moving.

In the game, Vice City will look like this forever. The real one won't.

Miami sits on limestone, a rock so full of holes it works like a sponge. When the sea rises, the water doesn't just come over the top. It comes up from underneath. That's why some streets in Miami flood on perfectly sunny days, during the highest tides of the year.

Local planners expect about two feet of sea level rise by around 2060. And NOAA has warned that this kind of flooding could hit Miami as often as fifty days a year by 2050.

Miami Beach is fighting back. It's raising roads, and spending hundreds of millions of dollars on pumps to push the water back out to sea. It might work for decades. Nobody knows if it works for a century.

So, why Florida? Because no other place packs this much into one story. A city started by a frost. A boom wiped out by a hurricane. Hotels saved with paint. A river made of grass. A crime wave turned into a TV show. And headlines that never stop.

Rockstar can rebuild Vice City whenever it wants. The real one is in a race against the sea.
