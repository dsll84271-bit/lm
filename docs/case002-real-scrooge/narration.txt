First, the fiction. In 1843, Charles Dickens introduced the world to Ebenezer Scrooge. He called him a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous old sinner.

Scrooge counts his money on Christmas Eve. He keeps his clerk, Bob Cratchit, freezing over a fire so small it looks like one coal. And when he's asked to give to the poor, he says, if they would rather die, they had better do it, and decrease the surplus population.

Then three ghosts show him his past, his present, and his future. And Scrooge changes.

Dickens never said who Scrooge was based on. But historians have a list of suspects. And the first is a man who sat in Parliament.

His name was John Elwes. Born in 1714, he inherited a fortune from his uncle, Sir Harvey Elwes, who was a famous miser himself. The two of them would sit together in the evening with one stick on the fire, sharing one glass of wine. And when it got dark, they went to bed. Because, as they said, going to bed saved candle-light.

Elwes became a Member of Parliament for Berkshire, and by the end of his life he was worth nearly eight hundred thousand pounds. In today's money, that's well over a hundred million pounds.

And here's how he lived. His friend Edward Topham wrote it all down.

He'd walk home through the rain, rather than pay a shilling for a coach. He'd sit in his wet clothes, rather than pay for a fire to dry them. He ate food that was rotting, rather than buy fresh meat.

One day, he dined on the leftovers of a moorhen, a bird that had been dragged out of the river by a rat. Another time, he ate part of a fish that had been found half-digested inside a bigger fish.

And Topham saw him pick a wig up out of a rut in a country lane. It looked like a beggar's old wig. Elwes wore it for over two weeks.

When he rode to London, he chose roads with no tolls. His rule was simple. Never pay a toll if you can avoid it.

Scrooge at least had a fire. In the Elwes house, the fire was allowed to go out at dinner, because eating, they said, was exercise enough.

Now, the darkest part. In his last years, Elwes owned more than a hundred houses. But he lived in whichever one happened to be empty. His furniture, Topham wrote, was a couple of beds, a couple of chairs, a table, and an old woman.

That old woman was his housekeeper. One day, Elwes's nephew couldn't find him. He traced him to an empty house, had the door forced, and found the old man upstairs, close to death.

Then they searched the attic. And they found the housekeeper, lying on the floor. She had been dead for about two days.

Elwes survived. Scrooge, remember, only saw his lonely death in a vision. Elwes almost lived it.

And Dickens knew his story. He mentions John Elwes by name in two of his later novels.

Suspect number two. Jemmy Wood, a banker from Gloucester. By the time he died in 1836, he was worth around nine hundred thousand pounds. People called him the richest commoner in the country.

He ran his bank with two clerks, out of a drapery shop. And at his funeral, his coffin was reportedly pelted with stones.

His will caused a legal battle that dragged on for years. One of his executors, consumed by the case, later took his own life.

And suspect number three. Daniel Dancer, who wore bands of hay instead of clothes. When he died, a fortune was found hidden around his home. Two and a half thousand pounds of it was buried in a dung heap.

Dickens owned these stories. In one of his novels, a character reads them aloud from a book called Lives and Anecdotes of Misers.

So, where did the name Scrooge come from? There's a famous theory.

In Edinburgh, Dickens supposedly saw a gravestone for a man called Ebenezer Scroggie. It described him as a meal man, a corn merchant. But in the fading light, Dickens read it as mean man.

It's a great story. But there's no evidence it happened. Historians have called it a probable hoax, and the gravestone itself was lost in 1932. On this one, the Detective's verdict is: unproven.

But here's where the case turns.

In February 1843, the British government published a report into child labour. It found that some children started work when they were only three or four years old. Many started at five. Most were in regular work by seven or eight.

Dickens was horrified. That year, he'd seen children working in the Cornish tin mines, and half-starved children at a ragged school in London.

His first plan was a pamphlet, called An Appeal to the People of England, on behalf of the Poor Man's Child. Then he changed his mind. He wrote to one of the report's authors, promising something with twenty thousand times the force. He called it a sledge hammer.

That sledge hammer was A Christmas Carol.

Look again at what Scrooge says. Are there no prisons? Are there no workhouses? Those workhouses were real. Under the Poor Law of 1834, the poor could be sent to them, families split apart, and made to work for their food.

And the surplus population? That was a real argument too. The economist Thomas Malthus had warned that the poor were multiplying faster than food. Scrooge wasn't just a miser. He was speaking the cold economics of his day.

In the book, the Ghost of Christmas Present shows Scrooge two starving children hidden under his robe. This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want. Dickens based them on the children he saw in that ragged school.

And now, the most personal clue of all.

When Charles Dickens was twelve years old, his father was thrown into a debtors' prison. Charles was taken out of school, and sent to work in a factory, pasting labels onto pots of shoe polish. Ten hours a day, for six shillings a week.

Years later, he wrote that he wondered how he could have been so easily cast away at such an age.

The boy who was cast away grew up to write the story of a man who learns to stop casting people away. He wrote it in six weeks. It was published on the nineteenth of December, 1843. And it's still being retold today. Including this year, with Johnny Depp.

So, let's look at the evidence.

Real misers, richer and stranger than Scrooge, really did exist, and Dickens knew their stories. The cruel ideas Scrooge spouts were real policies of the day. And the children in the book were based on real children Dickens had seen.

But there was no single real Scrooge. Dickens never named one, and the famous gravestone is probably a myth. Scrooge was a mix of real men. And the worst of him came from the real world.

So, how real is Ebenezer Scrooge? The evidence says… seven out of ten.
