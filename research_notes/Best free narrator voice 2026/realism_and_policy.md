## 1. Which free/open TTS voices are currently rated hardest to tell from a human (blind tests and community evidence)?

### Takeaway
The best blind-test evidence is the Artificial Analysis Speech Arena, which ranks models by Elo from crowd votes. In the snapshots I found, the top open-weights spots in late 2026 rotate between Fish Audio (S2 Pro, then "Drama 3"), Breeze TTS 2 and Ant Group's Ming-3.0-tts. ## 2. Best practices for cloning a consistent narrator (reference length, quality, transcript, seed, chunking)

### Takeaway
Clone quality comes mostly from the reference: about 10–15 s of **clean, uncompressed, single-speaker** audio in the target language and speaking style, with an **exact transcript** where the model supports one (Qwen3 ICL mode, F5-TTS). To stay consistent, build the clone prompt or embedding **once** and reuse it for every chunk. Chunk at sentence or short-paragraph level, never mid-sentence, and add deliberate pauses between chunks. Long single-pass generation tends to drift. Review quality at the paragraph level, not sentence by sentence.

### Cited Findings
**Reference audio:**
- Qwen3-TTS: ICL mode (`x_vector_only_mode=False`, with `ref_text`) is "strongly recommended for best quality". The text should accurately transcribe the reference audio. X-vector-only mode is faster but "may be less accurate" (it uses only a speaker embedding) — [Qwen3-TTS voice cloning docs (Mintlify mirror)](https://mintlify.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS/guides/voice-cloning)
- Qwen3-TTS: one guide says "the right model, 10–15 seconds of clean audio, and an accurate transcript" drive most of the clone quality — [ocdevel Qwen TTS voice cloning](https://ocdevel.com/blog/20260302-qwen-tts-voice-cloning)
- Qwen3-TTS **design-then-clone** workflow: VoiceDesign produces a slightly different voice on each call. The fix is to design the voice once, call the Base model's `create_voice_clone_prompt(ref_audio, ref_text)` once, and reuse that prompt for every line — [Qwen3-TTS README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-TTS/HEAD/README.md); [ocdevel](https://ocdevel.com/blog/20260302-qwen-tts-voice-cloning)
- F5-TTS uses the reference transcript to align the clip. A mismatched transcript degrades output and skews timing, because output duration = reference duration × (target text length / reference text length). Leaving `ref_text` empty triggers automatic ASR transcription at the cost of extra GPU memory — [F5-TTS infer README](https://huggingface.co/spaces/mrfakename/E2-F5-TTS/raw/630814d220ec334918462236ca35f5d96f09ceee/src/f5_tts/infer/README.md); [F5-TTS HF discussion](https://huggingface.co/SWivid/F5-TTS/discussions/12)
- Anecdote: an F5-TTS test with a 12 s clip from a compressed voice message found that the model **copied encoding artifacts as part of the voice**. The author recommended a longer, clean recording — [El Farssi blog](https://amineelfarssi.github.io/blog/voice-cloning-f5-tts-experiment/)
- Chatterbox: the reference should be in the same language as the text. Otherwise accent leaks in, and the mitigation is `cfg_weight=0` — [Chatterbox README (HF)](https://huggingface.co/ResembleAI/chatterbox/blob/refs%2Fpr%2F4/README.md); [Replicate readme](https://replicate.com/thomcle/chatterbox-tts/readme)

**Prosody and style controls:**
- Chatterbox defaults are `exaggeration=0.5`, `cfg_weight=0.5`. For calm, professional narration, a community guide suggests exaggeration about 0.3 with cfg 0.5. Dramatic reads use cfg about 0.3 and exaggeration ≥0.7. Higher exaggeration speeds speech up, which lower cfg offsets. With a fast-talking reference speaker, cfg about 0.3 improves pacing. Turbo ignores cfg_weight. (The MLX port lists a different default exaggeration, 0.1.) — [Chatterbox README](https://huggingface.co/ResembleAI/chatterbox/blob/refs%2Fpr%2F4/README.md); [Mintlify Chatterbox config guide](https://www.mintlify.com/yocxy2/chatterboxyocxy/guides/configuration); [mlx-audio docs](https://blaizzy.github.io/mlx-audio/models/tts/chatterbox/)
- IndexTTS-2 controls emotion three ways: an emotion reference clip, a text description, or an 8-dimension emotion vector, separate from speaker identity. Its paper describes an explicit token-count duration mode, but the Sept 2025 repo release said duration control was not yet enabled — [IndexTTS2 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.21619v2); [index-tts GitHub](https://github.com/index-tts/index-tts)

**Chunking and drift:**
- Qwen3-TTS paper: the model can synthesise more than 10 minutes of fluent speech in one go — [Qwen3-TTS tech report](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.15621). A later long-form TTS paper disagrees: Qwen3-TTS and VoxCPM2 reach state-of-the-art WER and speaker similarity on short prompts but "significantly deteriorate" on long-form input, because attention misaligns with the text — [Taming Long-form TTS (HF papers)](https://huggingface.co/papers/2609.16989.md)
- A Qwen3 long-form guide reports pronunciation and timbre drift across chunks in long narration. It found that the 1.7B model holds consistency over 10+ minutes better than smaller ones, and it recommends paragraph-aware splitting with sentence-length limits — [Medium: Long-form TTS with Qwen3](https://medium.com/data-science-collective/high-quality-long-form-tts-with-qwen3-open-weight-models-cdd6e3d00df0)
- Sentence-chunk concatenation loses cross-sentence coherence and leaves boundary artifacts — [MagpieTTS-LF](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.18485v1). Chunking "improves local content accuracy at the expense of long-range continuity", and WER and speaker-similarity metrics can't detect that stitching — [Emilia2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.13614)
- Each chunk is a fresh synthesis with no prosody carried over. Keep the same voice or prompt for every chunk, because mixing them causes audible seams. Split paragraph first, then sentence, and never mid-sentence — [Speechify audiobook pipeline](https://speechify.ai/blog/building-an-automated-audiobook-pipeline-with-the-speechify-tts-api)
- Use the same speaker embedding and prompt for every chunk, and add a fixed pause between chunks so each reset sounds deliberate — [BanglaBox](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.09211)
- Community suggestion: make paragraph breaks longer than sentence breaks (600–900 ms at paragraph boundaries) — [voicebox issue #363](https://github.com/jamiepine/voicebox/issues/363)
- QA should be done at paragraph level, because sentence-only checks miss intonation drift between sentences — [Bland.ai](https://www.bland.ai/blog/how-to-make-text-to-speech-sound-more-natural) (vendor blog)

### Inferences
- A practical recipe: (1) record or choose 10–20 s of clean, dry, calm narration-style male speech, WAV, no music, no reverb, no codec artifacts; (2) write an exact transcript; (3) build the clone prompt once and cache it; (4) generate in chunks of one to three sentences (or a short paragraph) with the same prompt and fixed settings; (5) render two or three takes of each chunk and keep the best (cheap, and catches the occasional glitch); (6) join with 250–400 ms sentence gaps and 600–900 ms paragraph gaps; (7) listen back paragraph by paragraph.
- The reference sets the delivery as well as the timbre. A calm documentary-style reference will give calmer output than an excited podcast clip.
- Setting a fixed seed makes reruns reproducible, but on its own it does not prevent drift. Reusing the same cached prompt or embedding is what holds the identity steady (inferred from the sources above).

### Gaps
- No authoritative data on the ideal reference length per model beyond about 10–15 s for Qwen3. The "under 12 s" figure for F5-TTS is not documented.
- Little concrete guidance on seed control was found. The Qwen3 long-form guide has a "voice consistency seed" section whose contents weren't retrieved.
- No controlled study comparing sentence-by-sentence and paragraph generation for **perceived naturalness** of narration.
- Fish Audio cloning best practices weren't retrieved, and its licence blocks commercial use anyway.

## 3. Post-production tricks that make TTS sound more natural

### Takeaway
The usual chain is cleanup → subtractive EQ (cut boxiness around 300–500 Hz) → light de-ess (around 5 kHz+, before compression) → gentle compression (2:1 to 4:1) → optional light saturation → a subtle room reverb or room-tone bed. Add or keep natural breaths and pauses at phrase starts. Pace documentary narration at about 130–150 WPM. Nearly all of this comes from audio-engineering and vendor blogs, not TTS research, so tune by ear.

### Cited Findings
- Chain order: noise removal → EQ → de-esser → compressor — [Voice.ai hub](https://voice.ai/hub/tts/how-to-make-text-to-speech-sound-less-robotic/). For AI vocals specifically: subtractive EQ → de-esser → compression → saturation → optional EQ/reverb send, with the de-esser before saturation — [Peak Studios, AI vocals](https://www.peak-studios.de/en/ki-vocals-bearbeiten/)
- De-ess lightly before compression, because compression makes sibilance louder. Optionally add a second light pass afterwards. De-essing only after compression needs aggressive settings that sound lispy — [Slate Digital vocal chain](https://slatedigital.com/how-to-build-the-perfect-vocal-chain-with-plugins/); [Voice.ai](https://voice.ai/hub/tts/how-to-make-text-to-speech-sound-less-robotic/)
- EQ: cut boxiness around 300–500 Hz, and make precise, moderate cuts rather than big boosts. Compression: moderate ratios (2:1–4:1) with gentle attack and release — [Voice.ai](https://voice.ai/hub/tts/how-to-make-text-to-speech-sound-less-robotic/); [Sonarworks](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/learn/how-to-achieve-a-natural-sound-using-vocal-effects-plugins)
- Breaths: add a short fade or breath sample at phrase starts to simulate breathing. Layer subtle room tone or reverb for warmth without masking the voice — [Voice.ai](https://voice.ai/hub/tts/how-to-make-text-to-speech-sound-less-robotic/)
- Avoid hard noise gates, which make the room "pump" and clip word endings. Avoid over-processing — [Sonarworks](https://www.sonarworks.com/blog/learn/how-to-achieve-a-natural-sound-using-vocal-effects-plugins); [lalal.ai](https://www.lalal.ai/blog/echo-removal-how-to-fix-roomy-voice-recordings/)
- Academic evidence is limited to intelligibility: compression plus high-pass filtering improves the intelligibility of synthetic speech in noise — [arXiv 2203.10637](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.10637)
- Pace: documentary narration is quoted at 135–150 WPM by one source and 120–140 WPM by another. General video narration is 130–160 WPM. A vendor sample of 53 long-form (mostly YouTube) voiceovers had a median of about 166.5 WPM. None of these is an industry standard — [Typecast WPM guide](https://typecast.ai/learn/?p=15068); [Syllaby pacing/retention](https://syllaby.io/blog/voiceover-pacing-silence-trimming-retention-editing/); [StudioBinder documentary narration](https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/narration-in-documentary/)

### Inferences
- Many modern TTS models already output a clean, close-mic sound. The most "AI" tells in documentary narration tend to be perfectly even pacing, identical gaps between sentences, and a bone-dry voice sitting on top of music. So **varied pause lengths**, a **very light room reverb or room-tone bed**, and **sidechain ducking of music by about 6–12 dB under speech** may do more than heavy EQ or compression. These are general mixing conventions; no source in this research quantified them.
- Keep breaths sparse and natural. Some newer models (and references that contain breaths) generate their own breaths, and adding more on top can sound fake.
- Loudness target: YouTube normalises playback (commonly cited as about -14 LUFS integrated). Not verified in this session.

### Gaps
- No controlled study or blind test measuring how much any single post step (breath insertion, reverb, EQ) improves perceived human-likeness of TTS.
- No source-backed values for music-ducking amount or YouTube loudness target were retrieved this session.

## 4. YouTube Partner Program rules on AI voices / inauthentic content, and how audiences perceive AI narration

### Takeaway
YouTube does **not** ban AI or synthetic voices in monetised videos. In July 2025 it renamed "repetitious content" to "inauthentic content", targeting mass-produced, templated, low-effort videos. A further clarification in mid-July 2026 split this into three categories: (1) generic or template-like content, (2) unsatisfying or off-putting content, and (3) AI personas giving advice on sensitive topics such as health, finance and law. Third-party coverage consistently says an AI or cloned voice reading an **original, researched script** with real editorial value is fine. What fails is TTS over stock footage or slideshows with no original analysis, and interchangeable listicle formats. Separately, realistic synthetic content must be disclosed. Audience surveys (mostly from vendors) show people trust human voices more, and trust drops when content is **perceived** as AI.

### Cited Findings
**Policy (official sources could not be fetched; based on news and secondary coverage):**
- July 2025: YouTube updated the YPP "repetitious content" policy, renaming it "inauthentic content" to target mass-produced and repetitive videos. Using AI tools does not by itself disqualify a channel from monetisation — [TechRepublic](https://techrepublic.com/article/news-youtube-ai-video-monetization-rules); [Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/software-apps/youtube-ramps-up-campaign-against-ai-slop-with-stricter-monetization-caveats-on-inauthentic-and-repetitive-videos); [eWeek: "AI is fine, as long as it's authentic"](https://www.eweek.com/news/youtube-responds-to-ai-concerns/)
- July 2026 clarification (reported 13–20 July 2026; one source dates it 16 July 2026): inauthentic-content guidance now names three categories: generic or repetitive content, unsatisfying or off-putting content, and AI personas related to sensitive topics. Per secondary reports, a YouTube trust-and-safety lead said AI personas "may be fine overall" but not when they discuss finance, legal or medical issues — [TechCrunch, 20 Jul 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/20/youtube-clarifies-policies-around-ai-slop-and-upsetting-videos/); [Tubefilter, 13 Jul 2026](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/07/13/youtube-inauthentic-content-monetization-policy-update/); [Engadget](https://engadget.com/2226445/youtube-explains-ai-slop-policy-why-some-creators-wont-get-paid/); [CreatorBlade](https://creatorblade.com/blog/youtube-inauthentic-content-policy-2026-stay-monetized)
- Creator-facing guides agree that faceless and AI-voiced channels are not banned. "An AI or cloned voice narrating an original script" is described as fine. At-risk formats are AI-narrated image slideshows without original commentary, TTS over stock footage without editorial analysis, and compilation or listicle channels where only the topic changes — [faceless.my](https://faceless.my/youtube/youtube-inauthentic-content-policy-faceless-ai-2026/); [viibeo](https://www.viibeo.com/blog/faceless-youtube-inauthentic-content-policy-2026); [vidIQ blog](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/youtube-ai-monetization/); [OutlierKit July 2026](https://outlierkit.com/blog/youtube-updates-july-2026)
- **Conflicting claim:** one news piece states that content "that can be easily created using generative AI, such as videos with synthesized voice, automatic frame cutting, or re-composed fragments of other people's videos" is ineligible — [The Star (Kenya), Jul 2025](https://www.the-star.co.ke/news/2025-07-17-youtube-ends-monetization-of-ai-generated-videos). Other coverage frames the issue as the underlying content, not the voice — [PCWorld](https://www.pcworld.com/article/2845159/youtube-cracks-down-on-ai-slop-while-enabling-ai-slop.html); [InVideo](https://invideo.io/blog/youtube-kills-ai-faceless-channels/). Treat the Star's framing as an outlier.
- Some sources say the policy was later renamed again to "Generic or Repetitive Content". Unverified — [search summary of creator blogs, e.g. Clippie](https://clippie.ai/blog/ai-videos-banned-youtube-2026-rules)
- **Disclosure (separate from monetisation):** creators must disclose realistic altered or synthetic content (e.g. making a real person appear to say something, or realistic footage of events that didn't happen). Clearly unrealistic content and minor aesthetic edits are exempt. Labels appear in the description, and in the player for sensitive or photorealistic content. YouTube may also apply labels automatically — [YouTube Help: Disclosing use of altered or synthetic content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491); [YouTube blog, Mar 2024](https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/youtube-says-will-require-creators-label-ai-content-rcna143937)
- Secondary report: YouTube treats AI dubs that recreate a creator's real voice as "AI-generated likeness". Not verified against YouTube docs — [Influencer Marketing Hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/youtube-inauthentic-content)

**Audience perception (industry surveys, not peer-reviewed, not YouTube-specific):**
- Adobe Express survey (850 consumers, 205 marketers; reported Mar 2026): 77% of consumers trust human voices most, 14% don't trust any voice they believe is AI, and 48% say AI audio's biggest problem is a lack of emotional authenticity — [StratCom Academy](https://stratcom.academy/2026/03/06/consumers-notice-ai-generated-voices-but-still-trust-humans-more/)
- Vocal Image study: listeners rated voices positively until told they were AI-generated, after which trust fell. UK listeners spot AI voices faster and trust them less — [ITBrief](https://itbrief.asia/story/uk-listeners-spot-ai-voices-faster-trust-them-less)
- Animoto (Sept 2025): 36% of consumers say AI-generated videos lower their trust in a brand — [Animoto State of Video](https://animoto.com/state-of-video)
- Raptive (2025, 3,000 US adults, reported second-hand): trust drops about 50% when content is perceived as AI-generated, whether or not it is. Could not verify against the original — [via Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtubes-ai-slop-problem-and-how-marketers-can-compete/567297/)
- Claim that talk-to-camera beats AI voice by 28–44% in engagement on Shorts/TikTok/Reels: no underlying data, treat as unverified — [Virvid blog](https://virvid.ai/blog/talk-to-camera-vs-ai-voice)

### Inferences
- For a documentary channel, monetisation risk depends on **originality and editorial value** (original research, scripting, sourcing, custom visuals and edit), not on whether the narrator is synthetic. Varying the format between episodes helps avoid the "template" signal.
- A plain AI narrator voice is not a "realistic depiction of a real person", so it likely doesn't need the altered/synthetic label on its own. Cloning a **real, identifiable person's** voice, or AI-generated realistic footage of real events, would. This interpretation is not confirmed by YouTube text.
- Viewer trust drops when they *detect* AI. That makes realism (natural pacing, no glitches, no robotic tells) commercially valuable beyond aesthetics. Avoid cloning a voice you don't have rights to: there are likeness and consent concerns, and YouTube's likeness-detection push points the same way.

### Gaps
- The official YPP policy text (support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392) and the July 2026 Creator Insider / YouTube blog post could not be fetched. Exact wording, effective dates and the "Generic or Repetitive Content" rename need checking.
- No peer-reviewed study of YouTube viewer reactions to AI **documentary narration** specifically, and no retention or CPM data comparing AI and human narrators.
- No documented list of named channels demonetised for AI voice was found. The examples are hypothetical formats from creator blogs.
