## Used in the 75-second test (hook + hour 1)

### T. rex top speed (the first catch)
- **Sellers et al. 2017, PeerJ 5:e3420** ("Investigating the running abilities of *Tyrannosaurus rex* using stress-constrained multibody dynamic analysis", University of Manchester). They found that running gaits would put unacceptably high loads on the skeleton, so the leg bones would break. That means T. rex was probably limited to walking speeds.
  - Paper: https://peerj.com/articles/3420/ (open access; also https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5518979/)
  - Reported top speed: about **20 km/h (12 mph)**. Sellers gave that figure to BuzzFeed, and Everything Dinosaur reports 20 km/h. Reuters reported "about 5 m/s ≈ 18 km/h".
    - https://www.buzzfeed.com/tomchivers/butiwouldratherbereading-surpriseme
    - https://blog.everythingdinosaur.com/blog/_archives/2017/07/18/t-rex-not-a-fast-runner.html
    - https://www.cbc.ca/lite/story/1.4210161
  - Other estimates run higher: up to about 28 km/h (https://www.snexplores.org/article/t-rex-may-not-have-been-able-run-it-was-still-pretty-fast). Earlier models span 5–15 m/s.
  - **On screen:** "≈ 20 km/h · AN ESTIMATE".
- **Walking speed:** van Bijlert, van Soest & Schulp 2021, *Royal Society Open Science* 8:201441 ("Natural Frequency Method"). They estimate a preferred walking speed of **1.28 m/s ≈ 4.6 km/h** (range 0.80–1.64 m/s).
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8059583/
  - https://phys.org/news/2021-04-dinosaur-biomechanical-tyrannosaurus-rex-gait.html
  - **On screen:** "≈ 4.6 km/h · 2021 STUDY".

### Cycling speed
- **Strava UK data** (reported by Cycling Weekly and BikeBiz). UK riders average about 23–26 km/h for men and about 20 km/h for women. A UK commute of 17 km takes about 43 minutes, which works out to about 24 km/h.
  - https://www.cyclingweekly.com/news/latest-news/british-cyclists-fastest-in-world-strava-303384
  - https://www.cyclingweekly.com/news/latest-news/10-stats-paint-picture-british-cyclists-compare-151542
  - **On screen:** "CYCLE COMMUTE ≈ 20–25 km/h". The narration says "roughly how fast people cycle to work".

### Size
- T. rex is about **12 m long and about 8 tonnes**. "Sue" is 12.3–12.8 m depending on the source. Mass estimates for large adults run from about 8 to about 9 tonnes (Hutchinson et al. 2011, *PLoS ONE*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3192160/).

## For the full video (day 1 → month 1 → twist)

### Day 1 — modern germs, no history of exposure
- Today's microbes have had 66 million years of evolution that dinosaur immune systems never met.
- There's no direct evidence for how a revived dinosaur would fare, so this must be framed as reasoned speculation.
- **Analogy:** "virgin soil" epidemics. Smallpox and other Old World diseases hit Indigenous Americans after 1492. Estimates of the collapse vary, from about 80–95% in some regions. Scholars dispute how much is down to "no immunity" versus conquest and famine, so cite it as an analogy with that caveat.
  - https://www.gavi.org/vaccineswork/what-or-who-wiped-out-most-indigenous-people-americas
  - https://brewminate.com/colonial-contagion-the-role-of-disease-in-the-collapse-of-indigenous-american-societies/
  - Counterpoint: ancient microbes are usually poorly adapted to modern hosts (https://www.webmd.com/a-to-z-guides/news/20230310/cm/zombie-viruses-fascinating-and-a-little-frightening). That's useful for an honest "but…" beat.
  - Dinosaurs did get diseases. The Poinars on parasites and disease load: https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/poinar-amber

### Week 1 — food: ferns and conifers vs today's plants
- Triassic and Jurassic plant-eaters lived on ferns, horsetails, conifers, cycads and ginkgoes. Flowering plants spread in the Cretaceous, and grasses appear only near its end.
- **Direct gut evidence:** the stomach of the nodosaur *Borealopelta* held about 85% ferns, and it picked particular ferns (Brown et al. 2020, *Royal Society Open Science*).
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7353971/
- Modern UK land is mostly grass, crops and flowering plants. Some species would be eating a menu they never evolved for.

### Month 1 — the cold
- **Late Cretaceous climate:** a greenhouse world with largely ice-free poles. Early Late Cretaceous tropical seas were above 35 °C.
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4082635/
  - https://www.earth.ox.ac.uk/2019/05/taking-the-temperature-of-a-late-cretaceous-greenhouse-world/
- **UK winter:** the mean is about 4 °C (1991–2020 average ≈ 4.1 °C; winter 2010/11 averaged 2.4 °C).
  - https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/blog/2025/winter-and-february-2025-weather-review
  - https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/binaries/content/assets/metofficegovuk/pdf/research/seasonal-assessment---winter26.pdf
- **Metabolism is debated:** Grady et al. 2014 (*Science*) argued dinosaurs were "mesotherms"; D'Emic 2015 disagreed. Say "scientists still argue about how warm-blooded they were".
  - https://phys.org/news/2014-06-mesothermy-mesozoic-untangle-energetics-extinct.html
