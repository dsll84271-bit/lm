# Episode 1: What If You Could Hear Every Lie?

**About 1,360 spoken words. That is 9 minutes at 150 words a minute before any pauses, so it clears 8 minutes even with a faster voice.** If the voiceover comes back under 8:30, slow the voice a little instead of cutting the silences. Lines in italics are picture notes and are not spoken. Lines tagged (check) are real-world claims to verify before you record.

**Cold open (0:00)**

*Kitchen, early morning. Mum at the sink with her back to us. The stickman at the table.*

You ask your mum how she slept. She says, "Fine, love."

And something rings in your left ear. High and thin, like a wine glass somebody has just flicked.

You ask again. "Honestly. I'm fine."

It rings again.

You don't know it yet, but your mum has just lied to you twice before breakfast. And from this morning on, you are going to hear every single one.

*Calendar page flips: DAY 1.*

**Day 1: the rule**

*High street. Shop. Office. Quick cuts, one ring each.*

You work out the rule by lunchtime. When somebody says a thing they believe is false, you hear the ring. Not when they're wrong. Only when they know.

Your mate says he's five minutes away. Ring.

The man in the shop says it's the last one in stock. Ring.

Your manager says, "No rush on this one." Ring. Loud.

At first it is the best day of your life. You feel like you've been handed the answers to a test that everyone else is still sitting.

And here is how big that test is. In the 1990s a psychologist asked people to keep a diary of every lie they told, however small. The average came out at one or two a day. (check)

One or two. Per person. Per day.

On a normal Tuesday you speak to about thirty people.

*A counter in the corner ticks up: 1, 9, 23.*

By the time you get home you have heard twenty-three.

There's one more thing worth knowing tonight. The machine people call a lie detector doesn't detect lies. It measures your pulse, your breathing and how much your fingers sweat. It detects nerves. (check) Which means that as of this morning, you are the only real lie detector that has ever existed.

**Day 3: what the lies are**

*Montage of small moments. Soft colours.*

By day three you've noticed something. The lies are not what you expected.

Almost none of them are wicked. They're tiny. "Love the haircut." "I was just about to text you." "No, you have the last one, I'm not hungry."

They are the bits of padding people put between each other so that nobody gets bruised.

And now you can hear every one of them tear.

*Calendar page flips: WEEK 1.*

**Week 1: the advantage**

*Car showroom. Bright, glossy, a little too shiny.*

Still. You'd be mad not to use it.

You buy a car. The salesman says it has had one careful owner. Ring. He says he can't go any lower. Ring. He says, "I'll have to check with my manager," walks round the corner, counts to twenty and walks back. You don't even need the ring for that one.

You save eleven hundred pounds in four minutes.

*Interview room.*

You sit in a job interview and you ask, "Why did the last person leave?" They say, "She wanted a new challenge." Ring. You thank them for their time and you go.

*Restaurant, first date.*

You go on a first date. She says, "I never normally do this." Silence. She's telling the truth. You like her immediately.

*Card table.*

You play poker with your friends exactly once. Nobody invites you again.

*Sofa. Television. The screen flashes red with every ring.*

You also discover you can no longer watch adverts. Or press conferences. Or anybody on a screen who is selling anything. Your living room sounds like a smoke alarm with a flat battery.

For one week you are the hardest person in the country to fool. And it feels incredible.

*Calendar page flips: MONTH 1.*

**Month 1: the cost**

*Birthday party. Warm light that slowly cools as the scene goes on.*

Then it's your birthday.

Your friends have organised it. There's a cake. Somebody has made a playlist. And one by one they come over.

"Wouldn't have missed it." Ring.

"You look great." Ring.

"We should do this more often." Ring.

Your oldest friend hands you a present he clearly bought on the way here. "Saw it and thought of you." Ring.

Here is the problem nobody warned you about. The ring tells you that a sentence is false. It doesn't tell you why.

It can't tell the difference between a friend who doesn't care, and a friend who is exhausted, skint, running late, and came anyway.

You hear the lie. You never hear the reason.

*The stickman alone at the table. Candles out.*

So you start filling in the reasons yourself. And you are not generous.

There is something else. You begin to notice how often it rings when you are the one talking.

"No, it's fine." Ring.

"I'm not annoyed." Ring.

"I'm really happy for you." That one is quiet. But it's there.

It turns out the person who lies to you most is the one you can't walk away from.

*Calendar page flips: MONTH 3.*

**Month 3: people find out**

*Office kitchen. People stop talking as the stickman walks in.*

You can't keep a thing like this secret. You're right too often.

Once people know, they change. They don't lie to you any more. They do something worse. They talk to you like a solicitor.

"I would say the meeting went broadly as planned."

"It's certainly a haircut."

"I have feelings about your girlfriend, and some of them are positive."

Nothing rings. Nothing is false. And nobody has told you anything at all.

You have made everyone around you honest. And you have never felt more lied to.

*Calendar page flips: MONTH 6.*

**Month 6: a very small life**

*Empty flat. Phone face down on the table.*

Six months in, your life is very honest and very small.

You've stopped asking people how they are, because you can't stand the answer. You've stopped asking, "Are we okay?" because you already know what you'll hear. It won't be the truth, and it won't be their fault.

People describe you as intense. They say it kindly. It rings.

*Sofa again. The girl from the first date, feet up, half asleep.*

The one person you've kept close is the girl from that first date. She tells the truth more than anyone you know. It's the reason you can stand to be near her.

One night you ask her, "Do you ever get tired of me?"

She says, "No."

Ring.

You don't sleep. In the morning you tell her you think you both need some space. That rings too. Only one of you hears it.

Researchers have a name for a lot of what you've been hearing. They call them prosocial lies: untruths told for somebody else's benefit. (check) Children start telling them at about three or four, around the time they work out that other people have feelings too. (check)

So the thing you've lost isn't the truth. You have more truth than anybody alive.

What you've lost is the benefit of the doubt. And it turns out most relationships are built out of that.

**The turn**

*Hospital corridor at night. Mum in a plastic chair. Everything still.*

Then your mum gets ill.

Nothing dramatic. A scan. A wait. A corridor with bad chairs.

You sit down next to her, and you ask the question you've been avoiding for six months.

"Are you scared?"

She looks at you. She smiles. She says, "No. Not a bit."

*Two full seconds of silence.*

And it rings. Louder than it ever has.

*Hold on the stickman's face. The ring fades into the hum of the corridor.*

And for the first time since day one, you understand what you're listening to.

That is not the sound of somebody deceiving you.

That is the sound of somebody protecting you. She's lying because you're her kid, and she would rather carry this on her own than hand you half of it.

It was the same ring in the kitchen. "Fine, love." It was always the same ring.

You take her hand. You say, "Yeah. Me neither."

And you hear it ring for you.

*Calendar page flips: YEAR 1.*

**Year 1: asking why**

*The kitchen from the opening. Evening light this time.*

A year in, the ring hasn't gone anywhere. You still hear all of them. Thirty, forty a day.

But you've learned to do the one thing the power never did for you. You've learned to ask why.

Your mate says he's five minutes away. Ring. You put the kettle on. He'll be twenty.

Your friend says she loves the present. Ring. You kept the receipt. You hand it over, and she laughs for about a minute.

Somebody says, "I'm fine." Ring. And instead of walking away, you sit down, and you say, "Okay. I've got time."

*Phone screen. Her name. A thumb hovering.*

And you call the girl from the first date. You tell her, "I asked you a bad question, and then I punished you for a kind answer."

Nothing rings.

She says she'll think about it. Nothing rings there either. For once, that's all you need to know.

You wanted to hear every lie because you thought the truth was hiding underneath.

It was. It just wasn't the truth you were expecting.

Most people aren't lying to take something from you. They're lying to give you something. A bit of peace. A good day. A birthday that feels like everybody wanted to be there.

*The stickman at the window. The camera pulls back through the glass and out over the street as the lights come on.*

So here's the question. If you could hear every lie, starting tomorrow morning, would you want to?

Be careful how you answer.

Somebody might be listening.
